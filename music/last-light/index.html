Last Light

A grunge song about the Sun turning into a black hole, and a countdown to the moment its last light stops arriving. One film, three takes of the same song.

The songBlack Hole Sun, but a real one

It started with Soundgarden's "Black Hole Sun" and one question: what if it were literal? What if the Sun collapsed into a black hole right now?

For 8 minutes and 20 seconds, nothing. The light already on its way keeps coming, so the sky stays bright. Earth doesn't even feel it: a black hole with the Sun's mass pulls exactly as hard, so the orbit never changes.

Then the light runs out, and not all at once. The middle of the Sun is closer to us than its edge, so the middle goes dark first. A ring of light shrinks to nothing in about 2.3 seconds.

The video joins with about two minutes of sunlight left and counts it down. Zero lands on the beat where the band crashes back in on "DARK!". The Moon follows 2.5 seconds later. Neptune keeps shining for another 8 hours.

A thought experiment: the real Sun doesn't have the mass to become a black hole. Everything else on screen is real physics, with sources.

Three versionsThree takes, one film

The song was generated several times from the same lyrics and the same composition plan, and three takes were kept. They sing the same words, each on its own clock.

So the film was drawn once, against M1, and re-timed to each of the others. Every cut, every lyric and every animation moves to where that singer actually is, and each countdown lands on that take's own crash. The counter always reads the real time left in the audio you are hearing.

How it was madeFive steps

  1. The physics first.Every number on screen comes from a sourced fact table (33 rows, from NASA, the IAU, gwosc.org and others). The countdown rounds up, so it never reads zero early.
  2. The song, then its clock.Generated with ElevenLabs Music, repaired by inpainting (a corrected Wow! line and a stop-time ending) and edited in code: a varispeed "redshift" over the bridge and a ring-out. Stems, forced alignment and beat fitting turn each take into one timing file.
  3. A simulated risograph.Every frame is drawn in code as four ink layers (black, fluorescent orange, blue and fluorescent pink on cream), then halftoned and misregistered like a photocopied zine. Four still plates come from an image model and are reprinted through the same inks; the black hole comes from a Schwarzschild ray tracer written for the film.
  4. A real chirp.The bridge riser is GW150914, the first gravitational wave ever detected, as recorded by LIGO Hanford: slowed 20 times and raised two octaves, with the merger landing on the bridge downbeat.
  5. Checked like a clock.Automated checks read each finished file: every lyric on screen on the frame it is sung, the countdown's digits read off the pixels, and the zero cut within one frame of the crash.

Sean set the brief and made the calls along the way: which takes to keep, no human characters, the look. Claude (Opus 5.5) built it in Claude Code.
