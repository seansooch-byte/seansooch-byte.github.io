Calcium, PTH and vitamin D

Blood calcium is held inside a narrow band. Follow parathyroid hormone from four tiny glands to bone and kidney, watch PTH make the kidney produce calcitriol, the active form of vitamin D that acts on the gut, then see what shifts in four conditions.

    Build the picture

    Calcium in the blood

    Blood carries calcium in three forms. A large part is bound to proteins, mainly one called albumin. Another large part is ionized calcium: free, electrically charged calcium (teal dots, Ca²⁺), the active form. A small share sits in complexes with small ions. Total calcium counts all three.

    Because so much calcium rides on albumin, a total calcium result is read alongside albumin. The Clinical values tab quotes the exact correction and the reference ranges.

    Build the picture

    When calcium falls, the parathyroid glands answer

    Usually four tiny parathyroid glands sit on the back of the thyroid, and their cells carry calcium sensors. When blood calcium drops, they release more of a hormone (a chemical messenger carried in the blood) called parathyroid hormone, or PTH: the amber hexagons.

    Build the picture

    PTH at bone: calcium comes out of the store

    Bone is the body’s calcium store. PTH stimulates osteoclasts, bone cells that break bone down and release its calcium into the blood. It also holds back osteoblasts, the bone-building cells, which spares blood calcium.

    Build the picture

    PTH at the kidney: less calcium lost in urine

    In the kidney, fluid filtered out of the blood flows along tubules and becomes urine. PTH makes the tubules reabsorb more calcium, taking it back from that fluid into the blood, so less calcium is lost in urine.

    Build the picture

    The kidney makes calcitriol for the gut

    Calcitriol (violet triangles) is the active form of vitamin D. OpenStax gives this step in two sentences:

    PTH → kidney → calcitriol → gut

    Build the picture

    Enough calcium turns PTH down; calcitonin

    Rising calcium inhibits further PTH release: negative feedback, a loop whose result switches off its cause. If calcium runs high, the thyroid’s C cells release calcitonin (blue squares), which inhibits osteoclasts, reduces gut absorption and signals the kidneys to reabsorb less calcium.

    Build the picture

    Phosphate: the kidney sends it the other way

    Phosphate is a mineral that bone stores alongside calcium (grey rings). When PTH breaks bone down, calcium-phosphate salts come out together. At the kidney PTH treats them oppositely: it keeps calcium but increases the loss of phosphate in urine.

    What changes it

    Primary hyperparathyroidism: PTH that ignores calcium

    In primary hyperparathyroidism a parathyroid gland overproduces PTH on its own, and keeps secreting it even when calcium is already high. Bone breakdown, kidney reabsorption and gut absorption of calcium all increase, so calcium climbs.

    What changes it

    Hypoparathyroidism: too little PTH

    In hypoparathyroidism the glands make too little PTH, most often after injury during neck surgery. Calcium falls and phosphate rises, and calcitriol, the active vitamin D the kidney makes, is decreased.

    What changes it

    Vitamin D deficiency: the gut absorbs less

    With too little vitamin D, the gut absorbs less calcium from food, and over time calcium stores are drawn out of the skeleton. It is a common cause of low blood calcium. The blood test that defines it, 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH D), reads in the deficiency zone.

    What changes it

    Decreased renal function: phosphate builds up

    The kidneys are the main exit for ions. When they work less well, as in chronic kidney disease (CKD), phosphate builds up in the blood: hyperphosphatemia. CKD is also a common cause of low blood calcium.
