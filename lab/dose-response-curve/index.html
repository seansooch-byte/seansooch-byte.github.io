Dose-response:
agonists and antagonists.

More drug fills more receptors, and the effect climbs until it reaches a ceiling. See how to read that curve, then watch a full agonist, a partial agonist, two kinds of antagonist and spare receptors each move it.

Educational, not for clinical use

Drug meets receptor

A receptor waits on the cell

Many drugs work by binding a receptor, a protein in the cell membrane that picks up a chemical signal from outside the cell. It has a binding site, a pocket on its outer face that only molecules of the right shape fit. Switched on, this kind (Lesson 31) hands the signal to a G protein, a partner protein waiting underneath that carries the message on inside the cell.

Drug meets receptor

An agonist binds and switches it on

A drug is a molecule that changes what cells do. An agonist is a drug that fits the binding site and switches the receptor on, like the right key turning a lock. The receptor changes shape, and its G protein switches on and moves off to carry the message. Binding does not last: the drug lets go, the receptor switches off, and it waits for the next molecule.

Drug meets receptor

More drug fills more receptors

A cell carries many receptors. The more drug around them, the more of them hold a drug molecule at any moment. The share of receptors holding the drug is its occupancy. Here every filled receptor adds the same push, so the effect, how much the cell's response changes, grows with occupancy. Once nearly every receptor is filled, more drug adds almost nothing.

The dose-response curve

Plot the effect against the dose

Plot the effect against how much drug is present, its concentration, and you get a dose-response curve. At first each extra bit of drug adds a lot of effect. Then the receptors run out, and the curve bends over and flattens. The curves in this lesson are illustrative: their numbers are shares, never doses.

The dose-response curve

Spread the doses out: a log scale

Useful concentrations run from tiny to thousands of times larger. On a log scale, each equal step along the axis means ten times more drug. The low doses squeezed into the corner spread out, and the same curve becomes an S: flat, then a steep middle, then flat again. Every curve from here on uses this scale.

The dose-response curve

The ceiling: efficacy

However much more drug is given, the effect stops at a top level: the curve's ceiling, also called its maximal effect. The height of a drug's ceiling measures its efficacy: how big an effect it can produce at all. Here the ceiling is 100%, the most this tissue can do.

The dose-response curve

Half the top effect: potency

The concentration that gives half of a drug's own top effect is its EC50, in the steep middle of the S. A lower EC50 means less drug does the job: the drug is more potent. Potency says where the curve sits from left to right; efficacy says how high it reaches. The two are separate.

Agonists and antagonists

Fully on, partly on, or blocked

Drugs that bind the same receptor can do different things once there. A full agonist switches each receptor it holds fully on. A partial agonist switches it only partly on, however tightly it binds. An antagonist switches nothing on and stops or reduces an agonist's switching on. This one holds the binding site; later steps meet one that acts from elsewhere.

Agonists and antagonists

Two drugs, one site

Binding comes and goes, so an agonist and an antagonist that fit the same site take turns at it. Raise the agonist and it wins more of the turns: here it goes from 1 receptor in 8 to 7 in 8, against the same amount of antagonist. An antagonist that shares the site this way is a competitive antagonist.

What changes it

Full agonists

Every full agonist switches the receptors fully on, so given enough, each one reaches the same ceiling. Here a second full agonist binds ten times more tightly, so it fills the receptors at a tenth of the concentration. Its curve slides left: it is more potent, with a lower EC50, but its efficacy is the same. More potent does not mean a bigger top effect.

What changes it

Partial agonists: a lower ceiling

Partial agonists can bind more or less tightly than a full agonist; this one fills receptors as readily as the normal agonist but switches each only half on. Even with every receptor filled, the effect stops at half the ceiling: lower efficacy, same EC50. Given with a full agonist, it takes over receptors the full agonist was using, so it can pull a strong effect down toward its own lower ceiling.

What changes it

Competitive antagonists: the curve shifts right

A competitive antagonist takes turns with the agonist at the binding site. At low agonist doses it wins most of the turns, so the effect is smaller. But enough extra agonist outnumbers it and fills the receptors anyway. The curve slides right, here to ten times the concentration for the same effect, and the ceiling holds.

What changes it

Noncompetitive antagonists: the ceiling falls

A noncompetitive antagonist takes receptors out of action in a way extra agonist cannot undo: it binds a different site, or binds for good. Here it disables 6 receptors in 10 in a tissue with no spare receptors. The rest fill and respond as before, so the EC50 stays put, but the ceiling falls to 40%, however much agonist is given.

What changes it

Spare receptors

Some tissues have spare receptors: more than their top effect needs, because each receptor's signal is multiplied inside the cell (Lesson 31). Here there are ten times more. The effect reaches half with only 1 receptor in 20 filled, so the curve slides left of the dashed curve of receptors filled. A noncompetitive antagonist here first slides the curve right; the ceiling falls only once the spares are gone.

    How this teaching model works

    Concentration is counted in units of the normal agonist's binding: at 1, it holds half the receptors. The share it holds, its occupancy, is its concentration divided by that concentration plus its binding constant. A competitive antagonist raises that constant in proportion to how much antagonist is there, because the two take turns at one site.

    Each receptor the agonist holds adds a push: a whole one for a full agonist, half for this partial agonist. A noncompetitive antagonist takes a share of the receptors out of action, and spare receptors multiply how many there are. The effect is the sum of the pushes, as a share of the most the tissue can do, and it stops at that top.

    In the normal tissue the top needs every receptor fully on, so the effect follows occupancy exactly. With spare receptors it is reached with only some receptors filled; the model draws that as a plain stop at the top, where a real tissue rounds the corner off. The model leaves out time, the drug's journey through the body and the body's own reflexes.
