The Lab / Unit 4: Hormones / Lesson 15

The HPA axis and steroids.

Two small glands on your kidneys make cortisol, and your brain keeps them in check with a brake, the same pattern as the thyroid. Build the loop one piece at a time, watch the brake work, see what too much or too little looks like, then watch why steroid drugs switch the loop off.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the loop

Two small glands on the kidneys

Your two adrenal glands sit like caps on top of the kidneys. A gland is an organ that makes and releases chemicals. Each adrenal gland has an outer layer, the cortex, wrapped around an inner core, the medulla. This lesson is about the cortex.

Build the loop

The cortex makes cortisol

The cortex releases cortisol into the blood. Cortisol is a hormone, a chemical messenger carried in the blood, and a steroid, one of a family of hormones built from cholesterol, a fatty substance. It helps the body cope with long-lasting stress.

Build the loop

Cortisol frees fuel

Cortisol tells the liver to make new glucose, the sugar cells burn for energy, partly from broken-down muscle protein, so more glucose enters the blood. That is why cortisol and its relatives are called glucocorticoids: steroids from the cortex that act on glucose.

Build the loop

Cortisol calms inflammation

Cortisol also turns down the immune system, the body’s defence against germs. That calms inflammation: the redness, heat and swelling of an immune response. More cortisol, calmer inflammation; less cortisol, fiercer.

Build the loop

A second steroid keeps salt

The cortex also makes aldosterone, the main mineralocorticoid: a steroid that manages minerals. It tells the kidneys to keep sodium, the main salt in blood, and pass potassium out in urine. The kidney’s own renin system sets it, far more than the brain does (Lesson 5).

Build the loop

The pituitary gives the order

The pituitary, a pea-sized gland at the base of the brain, sends ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) through the blood to the adrenal cortex. ACTH tells the cortex to make cortisol: more ACTH, more cortisol. It is the same pattern as TSH and the thyroid (Lesson 14).

Build the loop

The hypothalamus starts the chain

Just above sits the hypothalamus, a small brain region linking nerves to hormones. It sends CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) to the pituitary, which answers with ACTH. The three together are the HPA axis: hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal.

Build the loop

Stress turns the chain up

At the start of stress, such as illness, injury or hunger, the hypothalamus sends more CRH. ACTH rises, the cortex makes more cortisol, and blood sugar climbs to fuel the body. Even without stress, the chain rises and falls in a daily rhythm, highest in the early morning.

Build the loop

Enough cortisol puts on the brake

Cortisol in the blood reports back to the brain. When it runs high, the hypothalamus and pituitary send less CRH and ACTH, so the cortex slows and cortisol falls back. The result switches off its own cause: negative feedback, the axis’s brake.

Build the loop

Too little cortisol turns the order up

When cortisol runs low, the brake eases. The hypothalamus and pituitary send more CRH and ACTH and push the cortex harder until cortisol climbs back. So when the cortex itself is the problem, ACTH moves opposite to cortisol, just as TSH does to T4.

Build the loop

The whole axis: the key measures

Hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal cortex form one loop, held steady by the brake. Doctors read the loop from ACTH and cortisol measured together, and read its effects in routine blood tests: glucose, sodium and potassium.

What changes it

Cushing’s disease: too much cortisol

Cushing’s syndrome is the body under too much cortisol-like action, from its own cortisol or from steroid medicine, the most common cause. Shown here is Cushing’s disease: a pituitary tumor, an abnormal growth, pouring out ACTH that the brake barely slows. Cortisol and blood sugar climb; the brake pushes CRH down, but ACTH stays high or unexpectedly normal.

What changes it

Addison’s disease: the cortex fails

In Addison’s disease, primary adrenal insufficiency, the cortex itself is destroyed, usually by the immune system. Cortisol and aldosterone both fall: blood sugar and sodium tend to drop and potassium to rise. With the brake weak, CRH and ACTH climb high and push on a cortex that cannot answer.

What changes it

Secondary adrenal insufficiency: the pituitary fails

In secondary adrenal insufficiency the pituitary fails, so ACTH is low, or too low for the need, and a healthy cortex makes too little cortisol; blood sugar can drop. Aldosterone, run mostly by the kidney’s renin system, carries on, so potassium stays normal. ACTH tells the two apart: high when the cortex fails, low or unexpectedly normal when the pituitary does.

What changes it

Glucocorticoid drugs: the brake held on

Glucocorticoid drugs, such as prednisone, are lab-made cortisol relatives that calm inflammation and raise blood sugar. The brain reads them as cortisol, so the brake tightens: CRH and ACTH fall and the body’s own cortisol falls. After long use they are stopped slowly, or tapered, while the resting cortex wakes up.

What changes it

Mineralocorticoid drugs: replacing aldosterone

Mineralocorticoid drugs, such as fludrocortisone, act like aldosterone on the kidneys. In Addison’s disease they replace the missing aldosterone, so potassium falls back and sodium is kept. They do nothing for cortisol, so ACTH stays high until a glucocorticoid is added. Too much lowers potassium and raises blood pressure.

What changes it

Steroid synthesis inhibitors: the brake eases

Steroid synthesis inhibitors, such as metyrapone, block steps the cortex uses to build cortisol, and are used to treat Cushing’s syndrome; here, Cushing’s disease. Cortisol and blood sugar fall back, so the brake eases: CRH rises and ACTH climbs higher still, pushing on the blocked cortex.

    How this teaching model works

    Every level is worked out relative to the normal body and settles to a steady state. The hypothalamus’s CRH rises with long-term stress and falls in proportion to the glucocorticoid signal in the blood (the body’s own cortisol plus any glucocorticoid drug): that is the negative-feedback brake. The pituitary’s ACTH follows CRH and is braked directly too; a pituitary tumor adds ACTH that the brake cannot touch. The cortex’s cortisol output is its working capacity × the share of its steroid-building a synthesis inhibitor leaves (half) × the drive from ACTH, which saturates at three times normal. The loop settles where these agree, found by halving the search range seventy times. A failing pituitary (secondary adrenal insufficiency) scales ACTH down; aldosterone does not depend on ACTH in the model, so potassium stays put, as it does in the body. Every level follows the model’s physiology, including where a cited source gives no direction; the Clinical values tab says which directions are cited.

    Glucose follows the total glucocorticoid signal, and inflammation is calmed by it. Aldosterone output follows the working cortex, and a mineralocorticoid drug adds to it; more of this signal moves sodium up and potassium down. In the stopping chart, the cortex’s working capacity drifts toward what ACTH asks of it, so it shrinks while the drug holds ACTH low and recovers slowly afterwards. The sizes of all these effects, and the time axis, are illustrative choices; only their directions are meant to teach.
