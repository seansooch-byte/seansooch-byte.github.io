The Lab / Unit 5: The gut and drug handling / Lesson 17

Drug levels over time.

Swallow a tablet and its level in your blood climbs, peaks and fades. Take it again and again and the level settles on a plateau. Watch that curve draw itself, one piece at a time, then see what liver enzymes, weaker kidneys and a vein do to it.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the curve

A drug’s journey through the body

Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to a drug over time. A swallowed drug passes from the gut (stomach and intestines) into the blood and spreads around the body. Most drugs are then removed mainly by the liver, which breaks them down, and the kidneys, which send them out in urine; some also leave by other routes, such as bile.

Build the curve

The blood level

What matters is the drug’s blood level: how much drug there is in each drop of blood. Test a drop now and then, mark each result against time, and the marks trace a curve. Every graph in this lesson is that curve.

Build the curve

Swallowed: absorption

A swallowed tablet dissolves in the gut, and the drug crosses the gut wall into the blood: absorption. While drug comes in faster than the body removes it, the level climbs. The peak comes when the two balance; after it, the level falls.

Build the curve

The liver’s first pass

Blood from the gut flows through the liver before it joins the general circulation, the blood that goes round the whole body. Liver enzymes, proteins that speed up chemical changes, break some drug down (metabolism) on this first pass, and some may never be absorbed. The share of each amount that reaches the general circulation unchanged is its bioavailability.

Build the curve

Spreading out: distribution

From the blood, drug spreads into the body’s tissues: distribution. A drug that spreads widely has a large volume of distribution: less of the same amount stays in the blood, so its level is lower. If clearance is unchanged, less of it passes the liver and kidneys at any moment, so it also falls more slowly.

Build the curve

Removal: elimination

The blood keeps carrying drug past the liver, which breaks it down, and the kidneys, which pass it into urine: excretion. These two, with smaller routes such as bile, make up elimination. Clearance says how much blood is cleaned of the drug in a given time.

Build the curve

Half-life

For most drugs the body removes a fixed share of what is there, not a fixed amount, so the fall slows as the level drops. The half-life is the time for the level to fall by half. Each half-life halves it again: half, a quarter, an eighth.

Build the curve

After a few half-lives, almost none is left

Each bar is the drug left after one more half-life. After 4 to 5 half-lives only a few percent is left, so the drug counts as gone. A longer half-life stretches the whole fall out over more time.

Build the curve

Taken again and again: build-up

Many drugs are taken at regular times. Each new amount arrives before the last has gone, so it adds to what is left: build-up, or accumulation. The level does not climb forever, because the more drug there is in the blood, the more is removed in each gap.

Build the curve

Steady state: the plateau

In time, the drug removed in each gap matches each new amount. The level then rises and falls around a flat line: steady state. For an ordinary drug, removed a fixed share at a time, getting there takes about 4 to 5 half-lives, however much is taken, so a short half-life gets there sooner.

Build the curve

The working range

For some drugs the blood level guides treatment. Too low, the drug may not work well enough; too high, harm becomes more likely. The band between is the working range, or therapeutic range, and the aim is a steady state that stays inside it. Many drugs have no such useful range.

Build the curve

The whole picture: the key measures

Absorbed, trimmed by the first pass, spread out, then removed: the curve sums it all up. Three measures tell the story: the bioavailability, the half-life, and the steady-state level. Next, see what changes them.

What changes it

Enzyme inducers: cleared faster

Enzyme inducers, such as the herb St John’s wort, switch up the liver enzymes that break other drugs down. Less of a drug those enzymes handle survives the first pass, and the rest is cleared faster: a shorter half-life and a lower steady state, reached sooner, that can sink below the working range.

What changes it

Enzyme inhibitors: cleared slower

Enzyme inhibitors, such as the antibiotic erythromycin, slow the liver enzymes that break some other drugs down. More survives the first pass and the rest is cleared more slowly: a longer half-life, and a higher steady state, reached later, that can climb above the working range. Grapefruit juice blocks mainly the gut wall’s enzymes, so it raises how much gets in, not the half-life.

What changes it

Weaker kidneys: less removed in urine

When kidney disease leaves the kidneys passing less drug into urine, that is reduced kidney clearance. A drug the kidneys remove gets a longer half-life: it builds up for longer, so steady state comes later and higher, and can rise above the working range.

What changes it

Into a vein instead of by mouth

Intravenous means into a vein. Given as a quick injection, the drug skips absorption and the liver’s first pass, and all of it enters the general circulation at once: each amount makes the level jump straight up, usually to a higher peak. Removal is unchanged, so the half-life stays the same.

How to read these bars

The blue line on each bar, and the large number beside it, show where an illustrative teaching model sits: not a patient measurement and not a sourced value. Shaded bands and black typical-value marks are the reference values quoted under each row. Selecting a drug, condition or route shows the expected direction of each value from the quoted text and applies that state to the model.

How the half-life rows are read: the chapter’s numbers are counted in a drug’s own half-lives in a healthy body (“assuming a physiologically healthy individual”). The model keeps counting time in that usual half-life, so when a drug or disease changes the half-life, the markers show how much more or less is gone by then, and how much sooner or later steady state arrives.

    How this teaching model works

    One compartment, first-order everything. After one amount taken by mouth, the level is F × ka ÷ (Vd × (ka − k)) × (e−kt − e−ka·t); straight into a vein it is e−kt ÷ Vd. Amounts taken at regular intervals simply add up. F is the share surviving the first pass, ka the absorption rate, Vd how widely the drug spreads and k the elimination rate.

    Clearance is the liver’s part plus the kidneys’ part, CL = CLh + CLr. The half-life is proportional to Vd ÷ CL, the form of Equation 3 in the StatPearls half-life chapter (quoted with its constant in the Clinical values tab), scaled so that the normal teaching drug’s half-life is the model’s unit of time. The plateau’s average level is F ÷ (k × Vd × interval).

    Steady state is counted as reached once the level stays within one twentieth of its settled level at every point between amounts, from then on. The model works this out from the repeated curve itself, absorption included: a drug that is absorbed slowly takes longer to settle than its half-life alone suggests. The one-twentieth rule is this model’s choice, not a sourced value.

    Set both clearances to zero and nothing removes the drug: there is no half-life, a single amount stays in the blood for good, and amounts taken at regular intervals keep adding up with no steady state.

    The working range band is an illustrative zone drawn around the normal teaching drug’s plateau, not a real drug’s range. The lesson states change the model in the cited direction by illustrative amounts: an inducer raises liver clearance and first-pass loss, an inhibitor lowers both, reduced kidney clearance lowers the kidney part, and the vein route gives the whole amount at once. Left out: protein binding, a second tissue compartment, saturable (zero-order) removal, active breakdown products and every real difference between people. Nothing here is a prediction for anyone and nothing here is a treatment plan.
