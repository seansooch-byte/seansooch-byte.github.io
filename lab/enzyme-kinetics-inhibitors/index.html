Enzyme kinetics
and inhibitors.

An enzyme works faster as its substrate rises, until every enzyme is busy and the speed levels off. See why, then watch four kinds of drug bend that curve: one slides it right, two lower its ceiling, and one turns a dial on it.

Educational, not for clinical use

One enzyme at work

An enzyme speeds up one reaction

An enzyme is a protein that makes one chemical reaction run much faster, without being used up itself. The molecule it works on is its substrate; what comes out is the product. Acetylcholinesterase, for example, splits the nerve signal acetylcholine in two (Lesson 8).

One enzyme at work

The substrate fits the active site

The substrate binds in a pocket on the enzyme, the active site, whose shape fits it as a lock fits its key. Molecules of the wrong shape do not fit, so each enzyme works on its own substrate and little else.

One enzyme at work

Bind, change, let go, repeat

Once bound, the substrate is changed into product, here split in two, and the product lets go. The enzyme comes out exactly as it went in, ready for the next substrate. It can hold only one at a time, so each enzyme has a top pace it cannot beat.

The speed curve

More substrate keeps more enzymes busy

The reaction speed is how much product all the enzymes make each second. With little substrate, most enzymes sit empty, waiting. Add substrate and more of them are busy at any moment, so speed rises: here from 1 busy enzyme in 8 to 7 in 8.

The speed curve

Speed rises, then levels off

Plot speed against substrate. It climbs steeply at first, then bends and flattens: nearly every enzyme is busy, and the enzymes are saturated. The level the curve approaches is the maximum speed, its ceiling. More substrate cannot push past it, because there are no free enzymes left to use it.

The speed curve

The halfway mark: the Km

The substrate level that gives half the maximum speed is the enzyme's Km (the Michaelis constant). A low Km reaches half speed with little substrate; a high Km needs more. In this lesson's simple model a low Km means a readier grip on the substrate, but in real enzymes the Km also depends on how fast the bound substrate is changed, so it is not a pure measure of binding.

The speed curve

More enzyme raises the ceiling

Twice as many enzyme molecules make twice the product once they are all busy, so the ceiling doubles. Each one works just as before, so half speed comes at the same substrate level: the Km does not move. Fewer working enzymes do the opposite: a lower ceiling, the same Km.

Slowing it down

An inhibitor slows an enzyme

An inhibitor is a molecule, often a drug, that slows an enzyme. Some sit in the active site; others bind a second site elsewhere on the enzyme. Most are reversible: they bind and let go again. An irreversible inhibitor forms a lasting chemical bond, so that enzyme molecule never works again.

Slowing it down

Some enzymes have a control site

An allosteric enzyme has an allosteric site, a control site away from the active site, where the cell can turn it up or down. Many, like the one drawn here, are built from linked parts, each with an active site, and substrate in one part helps the others grip theirs. That gives an S-shaped curve instead of a plain bend; not every allosteric enzyme has one.

What changes it

Competitive inhibitor

A competitive inhibitor looks like the substrate and takes turns with it in the active site. Extra substrate wins the turns back, so the ceiling is the same. But here half speed now needs three times the substrate: the Km rises and the curve slides right. Statins slow the liver's cholesterol-making enzyme this way.

What changes it

Noncompetitive inhibitor

A noncompetitive inhibitor binds the second site, whether or not substrate is in the active site, and switches off each enzyme it holds. Substrate cannot push it off, so the ceiling falls, here to half. The enzymes left free work normally, so half of the new ceiling comes at the same substrate level: the Km stays put.

What changes it

Irreversible inhibitor

An irreversible inhibitor bonds to its enzyme for good. As it catches more molecules, the ceiling keeps falling, here to a quarter; the Km of those left is unchanged. Only newly made enzyme brings speed back. Aspirin does this to COX (Lesson 18), and organophosphate nerve agents to acetylcholinesterase.

What changes it

Allosteric modulators

An allosteric modulator binds the allosteric site and changes how readily the active sites grip substrate. A positive one slides this enzyme's S-curve left, here halving the substrate needed for half speed; a negative one slides it right, doubling it. The cell uses this as a dial: plentiful ATP slows an enzyme of sugar breakdown.

      How this teaching model works

      Substrate is counted in multiples of the plain enzyme's own Km, the level that gives half speed; speed is a percent of the normal maximum speed. A plain enzyme follows the Michaelis-Menten curve: speed equals the ceiling times substrate, divided by Km plus substrate. The allosteric enzyme here is a cooperative one: it follows an S-shaped curve with a Hill coefficient of 2.5, a measure of how strongly its parts help each other. Not every allosteric enzyme is cooperative; one that is not keeps the plain bend, and its modulators still shift it.

      More enzyme multiplies the ceiling. A competitive inhibitor multiplies the Km by one plus its strength and leaves the ceiling. A noncompetitive inhibitor divides the ceiling by one plus its strength and leaves the Km. An irreversible inhibitor removes its share of the enzyme from the ceiling for good. An allosteric modulator halves (positive) or doubles (negative) the allosteric enzyme's half-speed level.

      The double-reciprocal graph plots one over speed against one over substrate. A plain enzyme gives a straight line: it crosses the vertical axis at one over the ceiling and the horizontal axis at minus one over the Km. A competitive inhibitor steepens the line around the same vertical crossing; a noncompetitive or irreversible one raises it around the same horizontal crossing. An allosteric enzyme's line is curved. Real enzymes have more steps, inhibitors that do both, and substrate that runs out over time; the model leaves these out.
