A resting cell is negative inside because potassium leaks out of it. See why that voltage settles where it does, then watch high and low blood potassium move it toward or away from the point where the cell fires.

Educational, not for clinical use

Ions and charge

Ions carry charge

Salts dissolve in body water as ions: particles that carry an electric charge. Potassium and sodium each carry one positive charge, and chloride one negative charge. Inside cells, large proteins carry negative charges too, and they are too big to leave through the thin, oily cell membrane that wraps every cell.

Ions and charge

The pump keeps potassium inside

The sodium-potassium pump spends energy to carry sodium out of the cell and potassium in (Lesson 26). It keeps potassium about times more crowded inside than outside, and sodium about times more crowded outside. A difference in crowding across a membrane is a concentration gradient: ions drift down it, from crowded to sparse, wherever a door lets them through.

Ions and charge

A resting cell is negative inside

When a thin layer of extra negative charge lines the inside face of the membrane and extra positive charge the outside face, there is a voltage across it: the membrane potential, measured in millivolts (mV), inside compared with outside. In a resting nerve or muscle cell the inside sits at about mV in this lesson's model: the resting potential.

Ions and charge

Doors for ions

Ions cross the oily membrane only with the help of proteins set in it, such as the pump in step 2. The quickest are ion channels: protein doors that let particular ions through. At rest, many potassium leak channels stay open, while the sodium channels stay almost all shut. So a resting membrane lets potassium through about times more easily than sodium.

Ions and charge

A push and a pull

Two things act on an ion at an open channel. Its gradient pushes it from crowded to sparse: the chemical push. And opposite charges attract, so a negative inside pulls positive ions toward it: the electrical pull. The ion moves the way the stronger of the two sends it. Where they are equal, as many ions go in as come out.

Firing a signal

Threshold: the point where a cell fires

Nerve and muscle cells signal by firing. A rise of the voltage toward zero is depolarization. A small one fades away. One that reaches a trigger level, the threshold, about mV here, opens sodium channels in a rush: sodium floods in and the voltage shoots up, an action potential (Lesson 7). Then potassium carries the voltage back down to rest.

Firing a signal

The gap to threshold

How far the resting potential sits below threshold, about mV here, is the nudge a cell needs to fire. A smaller gap to threshold makes a cell easier to fire, more excitable; a bigger gap makes it harder. Anything that moves the resting potential moves this gap.

Firing a signal

Sodium channels: ready, open, inactivated

A sodium channel has two gates. At rest it is closed but ready. Depolarized to threshold, it opens for a moment, then its inactivation gate swings shut and plugs it. An inactivated channel cannot open again until the voltage returns to a negative resting level, which resets it to ready. The more negative the rest, the more channels stand ready.

Potassium in the blood

Blood potassium is the potassium outside cells

The fluid around cells trades freely with blood plasma, so a blood test for potassium reads the potassium outside cells. Almost all of the body's potassium is inside cells, so the outside amount is small, and a small shift changes it a lot. Too much potassium in the blood is hyperkalemia; too little is hypokalemia.

What changes it

Resting potential as a potassium diffusion potential

Open potassium leak channels and potassium drifts out, down its gradient, carrying positive charge; the negative proteins stay behind. The inside turns negative and pulls potassium back. Within moments the pull matches the push and the voltage holds: a potassium diffusion potential, about mV here. A slight sodium leak, partly offset by the pump, then lifts the true resting potential a few millivolts, to about mV.

What changes it

Hyperkalemia: a less negative rest

In hyperkalemia more potassium sits outside, so it is less crowded inside by comparison. The push out weakens, and a smaller negative pull balances it: the resting potential becomes less negative, about mV here instead of , and the gap to threshold shrinks from to mV. At first a smaller nudge fires the cell.

What changes it

Hypokalemia: a more negative rest

In hypokalemia less potassium sits outside, so the inside is even more crowded by comparison. The push out strengthens, and it takes a stronger negative pull to balance it: the resting potential becomes more negative, about mV here, and the gap to threshold grows from to mV. A bigger nudge is needed to fire, so nerves and muscles respond less, and muscles can feel weak.

What changes it

Sodium-channel inactivation when the membrane stays depolarized

Severe hyperkalemia holds the membrane partly depolarized, about mV here, so it never returns to a fully negative rest. Sodium channels that inactivate cannot reset, and about half stay plugged. With fewer ready, the upstroke, the action potential's quick rise, is weaker and slower: the cell becomes harder, not easier, to fire, and signals spread slowly. In the heart this can cause dangerous rhythms.

    How this teaching model works

    Potassium is held at a fixed level inside the cell, and sodium at fixed levels inside and outside; only the potassium outside, which a blood test reads, and the sodium leak change here. The settings follow Guyton and Hall's worked example of the resting potential and live in the Clinical values data file.

    Each ion's balance point comes from how crowded it is inside compared with outside: every tenfold difference is worth mV at body temperature. The resting potential is a weighted mix of the two ions, weighted by how easily each crosses the resting membrane (potassium about times more easily than sodium at the start), plus the pump's own few millivolts.

    Sodium channels stand ready at a negative rest. The share still ready falls along a smooth teaching curve as the resting voltage rises, half of them gone at about mV; the real curve differs between cells. All of these are the model's illustrative settings from the data file, not reference values.

    The model leaves out how hormones, insulin and acid-base changes move potassium between cells and blood, how the kidney clears it, and the fall in potassium permeability that very low potassium causes in some cells, which can make their rest less negative instead.
