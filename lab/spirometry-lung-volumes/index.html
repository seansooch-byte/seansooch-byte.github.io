The Lab / Unit 8: Lung volumes / Lesson 24

Spirometry and
lung volumes.

Breathe into a model spirometer, name every volume it can and cannot see, then blow one breath out as hard as you can. Watch its curves redraw in asthma, emphysema and fibrosis, after a bronchodilator, and with a weak blow.

Educational, not for clinical use

The volumes

A spirometer measures the air you breathe

A spirometer measures the air moving in and out through a mouthpiece, with the nose clipped shut. As the lungs fill and empty, it draws a spirogram: the volume of air plotted against time.

The volumes

A quiet breath moves only a little air

At rest, each breath moves the tidal volume (TV): the air that goes in and out with one quiet breath. The waves are small. Most of the air in the lungs stays there the whole time.

The volumes

Breathe all the way in, then all the way out

After a quiet breath in, you can still breathe in more: the inspiratory reserve volume (IRV). After a quiet breath out, you can still push out more: the expiratory reserve volume (ERV).

The volumes

Some air never leaves

Even after the hardest breath out, the residual volume (RV) stays in and keeps the air sacs from collapsing. It never passes the mouthpiece, so a spirometer cannot see it. Measuring it takes another test: a sealed body box (body plethysmography) or a harmless test gas that mixes into the lung.

The volumes

Capacities add volumes together

A capacity is two or more volumes added. Inspiratory capacity (IC) is TV plus IRV. Functional residual capacity (FRC) is ERV plus RV: the air left after a quiet breath out. Vital capacity (VC) is all the air you can move. Total lung capacity (TLC) is VC plus RV.

The volumes

At FRC, the lung's pull and the chest's spring balance

The lung is elastic: stretched, it pulls inward. That pull is its elastic recoil. The chest wall springs outward. The pleura, two thin layers sealed by a film of fluid, holds the lung against the wall, so the two move as one. At the end of a quiet breath out the two balance, and that point is FRC. A lung that pulls harder, or more weakly, moves the balance point.

Blow it out fast

A forced breath out: FVC and FEV1

Now breathe all the way in, then blow out as hard and as fast as you can until nothing is left. The total blown out is the forced vital capacity (FVC). The part blown out in the first second is the forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1).

Blow it out fast

The ratio: how much leaves in the first second

Divide FEV1 by FVC to get the FEV1/FVC ratio: the share of the breath that leaves in the first second. Healthy lungs blow most of it out in that second. Whether a ratio is low is judged against the lower limit of normal, which depends on the person's age, sex and height.

Blow it out fast

The same breath, drawn as a loop

Plot flow, how fast air moves, against the volume blown out, and you get a flow-volume loop. Flow shoots up early to the peak expiratory flow, then falls in a nearly straight line as the lung empties. Breathing back in draws the lower half.

Blow it out fast

Past the peak, pushing harder does not help

A harder push gives a higher peak. But after the peak, the same squeeze that pushes air out also presses on the airways and narrows them, so flow stops rising with effort. The downslope is set by the lung's recoil and the width of its airways, not by how hard you push.

Blow it out fast

Two questions: slow to empty, or small?

An obstructive pattern means air is slow to get out: FEV1 falls more than FVC, so the ratio is low. A restrictive pattern means the lung is small: TLC is low, and FEV1 and FVC fall together, so the ratio is normal or high. Proving TLC is low needs the body box or gas test.

What changes it

Asthma: narrowed airways

In asthma, the airway muscle tightens and the airway lining swells and makes mucus, so the airways narrow. Air leaves slowly: FEV1 falls far more than FVC, and the ratio drops. The loop's peak falls and its downslope scoops inward. Air trapped behind narrow airways raises RV.

What changes it

Emphysema: a floppy lung that cannot recoil

In emphysema, most often from smoking, the walls between air sacs are destroyed. The lung stretches easily but recoils weakly, and small airways that those walls held open collapse during the blow. Emptying is slow, the ratio falls and the loop scoops deeply. RV, FRC and TLC rise: the lung is overinflated.

What changes it

Fibrosis: a stiff, small lung

In pulmonary fibrosis, scar tissue stiffens the lung. It pulls inward harder and cannot fill as far, so every volume shrinks: TLC, VC, FRC and RV all fall. FEV1 and FVC fall together, and the strong recoil empties the lung quickly, so the ratio is normal or high. The loop is narrow.

What changes it

A bronchodilator reopens narrowed airways

A bronchodilator, such as an inhaled beta-2 agonist, relaxes airway muscle. The test is repeated after it. In this asthma, FEV1 and the peak rise and less air is trapped. A rise above the guideline's threshold is a significant response; it supports asthma, but on its own it neither proves asthma nor rules out COPD.

What changes it

Poor effort: the curves fall, the lung does not

A slow start, or a blow that stops early, gives a low, late, rounded peak and a short FVC. The lung has not changed: its volumes are the same. Because poor effort can look like disease, a test counts only when repeated good blows agree. Check the effort before reading the pattern.

How to read these bars

Each bar shows the cited textbook range as a shaded band, or a single typical value as a black tick. The blue line is the illustrative teaching model with the setting you chose, not a patient measurement. The model's normal lung is built from the textbook tidal volume, residual volume and FRC, with the middle of the vital capacity range.

FEV1/FVC is reported against the lower limit of normal as a z-score, which needs reference equations for the person's age, sex and height; the model gives only a plain fraction, so that bar has no blue line. The bronchodilator threshold is a change, counted as a share of the person's predicted value, not of their own starting value.

    How this teaching model works

    The normal lung takes its tidal volume, residual volume and FRC from the Clinical values data file, and its vital capacity from the middle of that file's range; TLC is VC plus RV. Stretchiness (compliance) below normal shrinks every volume, as in fibrosis (the push stays the same but drives a smaller lung, so the peak flow ends a little below normal); above normal it raises RV most, FRC less and TLC least, as in emphysema, so VC falls a little. Narrowed airways trap some air, raising RV and FRC.

    The forced breath is stepped forward in time. Flow is the smaller of two limits: what the chest muscles can drive, which rises over the first fraction of a second and scales with effort, and what the lung can deliver at the volume left, which falls as the lung empties. That second limit is the downslope: it depends on recoil and airway width, not on effort. Its emptying time is a model setting, chosen so the normal model blows out most of its breath in the first second; narrowed airways and weak recoil lengthen it and bow the downslope inward, a stiff lung shortens it. A poor blow starts slowly and fades early.

    The model leaves out the chest wall's own stiffness, airway collapse as a separate mechanism, gas trapping that changes from breath to breath, reference equations and z-scores. The sizes of every change are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.
