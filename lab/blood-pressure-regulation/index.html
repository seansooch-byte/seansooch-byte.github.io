The Lab / Core medicine / Lesson 4

Blood pressure
& how it holds steady.

Pressure is what you get when the heart pushes blood into narrow vessels that hold it back. Follow how a fast nerve reflex keeps it steady from one moment to the next.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

Blood travels in a loop

The heart pumps blood into arteries, the vessels that carry blood away from the heart. Blood passes through the body’s tissues and comes back through veins, the vessels that return it to the heart.

Build the idea

Pressure is the push

Blood pressure is the push of blood against the artery walls. That push is what drives blood on through the tissues. The dial shows it: further right means a stronger push.

Build the idea

It rises and falls with every beat

Each squeeze sends a surge into the arteries. The peak push is the systolic pressure; the low point just before the next beat is the diastolic pressure. The average over time is the mean pressure.

Build the idea

Flow: what the heart sends out

Cardiac output is the blood the heart pumps each minute: how often it beats, times how much leaves with each beat. The Frank–Starling lesson shows what sets each beat.

Build the idea

Resistance: the narrow ends

Arterioles are the smallest arteries, wrapped in muscle. When that muscle tightens they narrow and raise resistance: how hard it is for blood to get through. When it relaxes they widen and resistance falls.

Build the idea

Pressure comes from flow meeting resistance

Think of a garden hose. Turn the tap up, or pinch the nozzle, and the pressure inside rises. Blood pressure works the same way: more flow, or more resistance, means more pressure. This is the key idea of the lesson.

Build the idea

Volume fills the system

Blood volume is how much blood the circulation holds. More volume fills the veins, more blood returns to the heart, and each beat can pump more. Over days the kidneys set it: see the nephron lesson.

Build the idea

Sensors in the artery wall

Baroreceptors are stretch sensors in the walls of the large arteries in the neck and chest. Higher pressure stretches them more, so they send faster signals along a nerve to the brainstem, the base of the brain.

Build the idea

The brain answers through nerves

The brainstem sets the sympathetic nerves, the body’s “speed up” nerves. More of their signal makes the heart beat faster and squeeze harder, and tightens arterioles and veins. Another nerve, the vagus nerve, slows the heart.

Build the idea

A reflex that steadies pressure

The baroreflex is this sensor-to-nerve loop working by itself. Stand up and blood pools in the legs, so pressure dips. The sensors fire less, sympathetic signals rise, and within seconds pressure recovers most of the way.

Build the idea

The whole picture

Pressure comes from flow meeting resistance. Flow depends on heart rate, each beat and blood volume. The baroreflex steadies pressure from moment to moment; the kidneys set its long-term level. Key measures: mean pressure, cardiac output and resistance.

What changes it

Calcium channel blockers: arterioles relax

Calcium channel blockers cut the calcium that arteriole muscle needs to tighten. Arterioles widen, resistance falls and pressure drops. The baroreflex speeds the heart a little; some drugs in this class instead slow the heart.

What changes it

Alpha-1 blockers: nerve signals lose their grip

Alpha-1 blockers stop sympathetic signals from tightening arterioles and veins. Resistance falls and less blood is pushed back to the heart, so pressure drops, especially on standing up. The heart speeds up a little as the reflex responds.

What changes it

Central alpha-2 agonists: a quieter brainstem

An agonist is a drug that switches on a receptor, a cell’s docking site for a signal. Central alpha-2 agonists switch on receptors in the brainstem that turn sympathetic signals down. The heart slows, arterioles relax and flow falls, so pressure drops.

What changes it

Beta-blockers: a slower, gentler heart

Beta-blockers block sympathetic signals at the heart, so it beats more slowly and squeezes less. Flow falls, and so does pressure. At first the reflex tightens arterioles a little, which is why resistance rises in this picture.

What changes it

Direct vasodilators: arterioles wide open

Direct vasodilators relax arteriole muscle directly. Resistance falls a lot and pressure drops, so the baroreflex turns sympathetic signals up: the heart races and flow rises. Over days the kidneys also hold on to salt and water.

What changes it

Hemorrhage: less blood to pump

Hemorrhage means losing blood. Less blood returns to the heart, so each beat carries less and flow falls. The baroreflex speeds the heart and tightens arterioles, so pressure falls less than flow does.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is relative to a normal resting state. A sympathetic signal leaves the brainstem; with the baroreflex working it rises when pressure is below normal and falls when it is above. The heart’s share of that signal sets heart rate and contraction strength; the vessels’ share tightens arterioles (resistance) and firms up veins, which pushes more blood back to the heart. Blood per beat rises with filling and levels off. Flow is heart rate times blood per beat, and mean pressure is flow times resistance. The model finds the steady state where the reflex and the circulation agree. It omits the kidneys, hormones, pulse-wave shape and anything slower than minutes. The numbers shown are in physical units built from the textbook heart rate, stroke volume and mean pressure in the Clinical values tab: flow in mL/s is heart rate (beats per minute) times stroke volume (mL per beat), divided by 60 seconds per minute, and mean pressure is that flow times resistance (venous pressure taken as zero). The Clinical values tab explains where this baseline differs from the textbook references.
