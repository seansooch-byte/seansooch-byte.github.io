The Lab / Unit 1: The heart, the kidney and blood pressure / Lesson 4

Blood pressure
& how it holds steady.

Pressure is what you get when the heart pushes blood into narrow vessels that hold it back. Follow how a fast nerve reflex keeps it steady from one moment to the next, then watch what each blood pressure drug moves, and how the reflex answers.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

Blood travels in a loop

The heart pumps blood into arteries, the vessels that carry blood away from the heart. It passes through tiny vessels in the body’s tissues, then comes back through veins, the vessels that return it to the heart. Round and round, without stopping.

Build the idea

Pressure is the push

Blood pressure is the push of blood against the artery walls. It is much higher in the arteries than in the veins, and that difference is what drives blood on through the tissues. The dial shows the push: further right means a stronger push.

Build the idea

It rises and falls with every beat

Each squeeze sends a surge into the arteries. The peak push is the systolic pressure; the low point just before the next beat is the diastolic pressure, held up between beats as the stretched arteries recoil. The average over time is the mean pressure, nearer the low point, because the low part of each beat lasts longer.

Build the idea

Flow: what the heart sends out

Cardiac output is the blood the heart pumps each minute: how often it beats, the heart rate, times how much leaves with each beat, the stroke volume. It is the flow the heart sends into the arteries. Lesson 3 shows what sets each beat.

Build the idea

Resistance: the narrow ends

Arterioles are the smallest arteries, wrapped in muscle. When that muscle tightens they narrow and raise resistance: how hard it is for blood to get through. When it relaxes they widen and resistance falls. Arterioles set most of the body’s resistance.

Build the idea

Pressure comes from flow meeting resistance

Think of a garden hose. Turn the tap up, or pinch the nozzle, and the pressure inside rises. Blood pressure works the same way: mean pressure is flow times resistance. More flow, or more resistance, means more pressure. This is the key idea of the lesson.

Build the idea

Volume fills the system

Blood volume is how much blood the circulation holds; most of it sits in the stretchy veins. More volume fills the veins, more blood returns to the heart, and each beat pumps more, so flow and pressure rise. Over days the kidneys set it: see Lesson 2.

Build the idea

Sensors in the artery wall

Baroreceptors are stretch sensors in the walls of the large arteries in the neck and chest. Higher pressure stretches them more, so they send signals faster along their nerves to the brainstem, the base of the brain. Lower pressure, slower signals.

Build the idea

The brain answers through nerves

The brainstem answers through two sets of nerves. Sympathetic nerves, the body’s “speed up” nerves, make the heart beat faster and squeeze harder, tighten arterioles and firm up veins. The vagus nerve slows the heart.

Build the idea

A reflex that steadies pressure

The baroreflex is this sensor-to-nerve loop, working on its own. Stand up and blood pools in the legs, so pressure dips. The sensors fire less, the brainstem sends more sympathetic signal and less through the vagus, and within seconds pressure recovers most of the way.

Build the idea

The whole picture

Pressure is flow times resistance. Flow is heart rate times blood per beat, and blood volume fills each beat. The baroreflex steadies pressure within seconds; the kidneys set its long-term level over days. Each change ahead moves these three bars away from their dashed normal levels.

What changes it

Calcium channel blockers: arterioles relax

Calcium channel blockers cut the calcium that arteriole muscle needs to tighten. The artery-relaxing kind, dihydropyridines such as amlodipine, widen arterioles: resistance falls and pressure drops. The baroreflex answers by speeding the heart a little. The other kind, verapamil and diltiazem, also act on the heart and slow it instead.

What changes it

Alpha-1 blockers: nerve signals lose their grip

Alpha-1 blockers, such as prazosin, stop sympathetic signals from tightening arterioles and veins. Resistance falls and relaxed veins hold more blood, so pressure drops, most of all on standing up. The reflex speeds the heart a little, which keeps flow up; it cannot tighten the vessels, because their receptors are blocked.

What changes it

Central alpha-2 agonists: a quieter brainstem

An agonist is a drug that switches on a receptor, a cell’s docking site for a signal. Central alpha-2 agonists, such as clonidine, switch on brainstem receptors that turn sympathetic signals down. The heart slows, flow falls and arterioles relax, so pressure drops. The heart does not race: the drug works in the reflex’s own centre.

What changes it

Beta-blockers: a slower, gentler heart

Beta-blockers block sympathetic signals at the heart, so it beats more slowly and squeezes a little less. Flow falls, and so does pressure. The reflex cannot speed the blocked heart, so it tightens arterioles a little instead: at first, resistance rises.

What changes it

Direct vasodilators: arterioles wide open

Direct vasodilators, such as hydralazine, relax arteriole muscle directly. Resistance falls a lot and pressure drops, so the baroreflex turns sympathetic signals up: the heart races, called reflex tachycardia, and flow rises. Over days the kidneys also hold on to salt and water.

What changes it

Hemorrhage: less blood to pump

Hemorrhage means losing blood. Less blood returns to the heart, so each beat carries less and flow falls. The baroreflex speeds the heart and tightens arterioles, raising resistance, so pressure falls less than flow does.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is relative to a normal resting state. A sympathetic signal leaves the brainstem; with the baroreflex working it rises when pressure is below the level the brainstem defends and falls when it is above. Turning the brainstem’s sympathetic setting down, as a central alpha-2 agonist does, lowers both the signal and that defended level, so the reflex does not fight back. The heart’s share of the signal sets heart rate and adds a little contraction strength; the vessels’ share tightens arterioles (resistance) and firms up veins, which pushes more blood back to the heart. Blood per beat rises with filling and levels off, and a slower heart fills a little longer, as in Lesson 3. Flow is heart rate times blood per beat, and mean pressure is flow times resistance. The model finds the steady state where the reflex and the circulation agree. It omits the kidneys, hormones, pulse-wave shape and anything slower than minutes. The numbers shown are in physical units built from the textbook heart rate, stroke volume and mean pressure in the Clinical values tab: flow in mL/s is heart rate (beats per minute) times stroke volume (mL per beat), divided by 60 seconds per minute, and mean pressure is that flow times resistance (venous pressure taken as zero). The Clinical values tab explains where this baseline differs from the textbook references.
