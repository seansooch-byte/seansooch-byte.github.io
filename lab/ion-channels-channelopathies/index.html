The Lab / Unit 9: Cells, membranes and signals / Lesson 30

Ion channels
and channelopathies.

A channel is a gated pore through the cell membrane. Build one, filter and gate, then follow two broken pores: chloride that cannot leave the airway in cystic fibrosis, and a heart muscle cell that cannot finish repolarizing in congenital long QT.

Educational, not for clinical use

A gated pore

The membrane keeps ions apart

Every cell is wrapped in a thin, oily skin, the cell membrane. Salts dissolve in body water as ions, tiny particles that carry an electric charge: sodium and chloride crowd outside the cell, potassium inside. Charged particles cannot pass through the oily middle of the membrane, so on their own they bounce back and stay where they are.

A gated pore

A channel is a tunnel through it

An ion channel is a protein that spans the membrane, with a narrow, water-filled tunnel down its middle, the pore. Ions slip through the pore one after another, millions every second, far faster than a pump could carry them.

A gated pore

Each channel picks its ion

Near its outer mouth the pore narrows to a selectivity filter, lined so that only one kind of ion fits comfortably. A potassium channel lets potassium through but turns sodium away, though sodium is smaller. A chloride channel passes chloride. Each channel is named for the ion it passes.

A gated pore

A gate opens and shuts the pore

Most channels are not open all the time. A gate, a part of the protein that twists or swings, shuts the pore or opens it. Shut, nothing passes; open, ions stream through. A channel flicks between the two, and what counts is how much of the time it spends open.

A gated pore

Open, ions run downhill

Two forces move an ion through an open pore: crowding pushes it from crowded to scarce, and charge pulls it toward the opposite charge. They can pull opposite ways; together they make its electrochemical gradient, and the ion flows down it, spending no energy. For potassium in a resting cell, crowding wins, so it flows out. That flow of charge is an electrical current: more channels open, bigger current.

A gated pore

What opens the gate

Channels answer to different triggers. A voltage-gated channel carries charged sensors that move when the voltage across the membrane changes, pulling its gate open. A ligand-gated channel opens when a messenger molecule, its ligand, binds to it, as acetylcholine does on a muscle (Lesson 8).

A gated pore

A second gate: inactivation

The fast sodium channel of nerve and heart muscle has two gates (Lesson 28). A rise in voltage opens it and sodium pours in. Within about a thousandth of a second a second gate, the inactivation gate, swings into the pore from inside and plugs it. This inactivation keeps each sodium surge brief.

Chloride and the airway

The airway is lined by a thin wet film

The airways are lined by cells topped with cilia, tiny hairs that beat together. They stand in a thin, watery film, the airway surface liquid. On it floats a layer of sticky mucus that traps dust and germs. The beating cilia sweep the mucus up and out of the lungs, to be swallowed or coughed up.

Chloride and the airway

CFTR: the airway's chloride channel

CFTR is a chloride channel on the airway face of these cells. Its gate opens when the cell tags it with phosphate and two molecules of ATP, the cell's energy carrier, bind its two inner domains and pull them together. Carriers on the cell's far side load it with chloride, so when CFTR opens, chloride flows out.

Chloride and the airway

Chloride out, water follows

Chloride leaving carries negative charge into the airway liquid, and positive sodium follows it, slipping between the cells. That salt pulls water after it by osmosis, water moving toward more dissolved particles (Lesson 25). So the liquid stays deep enough for the cilia to beat freely, and the mucus thin enough to move.

Potassium and the heartbeat

A heart muscle cell's action potential

In a heart muscle cell each beat is one action potential (Lesson 7): sodium channels open and the voltage leaps up; calcium channels hold it high in a long plateau while the cell squeezes; then potassium channels bring it back down to rest. That fall back is repolarization.

Potassium and the heartbeat

Potassium channels end the beat

Repolarization is the work of potassium channels that open slowly during the plateau. Potassium flowing out of the cell through them, the repolarizing potassium current, takes positive charge away, so the voltage falls. In the ventricles two channels carry most of it, named for their genes: KCNQ1 and KCNH2.

Potassium and the heartbeat

In against out sets the length

Through the plateau two currents pull against each other: calcium flowing in holds the voltage up; potassium flowing out pulls it down. As calcium channels close and potassium channels open, outward wins and the voltage falls. Weaken the outward current, or add inward current, and the plateau lasts longer. On an ECG the action potential's length shows as the QT interval.

What changes it

CFTR failure: cystic fibrosis

In cystic fibrosis both copies of the CFTR gene are faulty. In the commonest fault the protein misfolds and the cell destroys it; others leave a gate that barely opens. Here chloride flow falls to almost nothing, so sodium and water stop following. The liquid thins to a third, the cilia lie flat and thick mucus stays put: infections follow. Sweat is saltier, the basis of the sweat test.

What changes it

CFTR modulators

CFTR modulators are drugs that act on the faulty protein itself. A corrector helps it fold, so more channels reach the membrane; a potentiator holds their gates open longer. Together, for the faults they suit, they bring back part of the chloride flow, here about 60 percent of normal: the liquid deepens, the cilia beat again and the mucus moves, about half as fast as normal.

What changes it

Congenital long QT: a potassium channel fails

In congenital long QT types 1 and 2, a child inherits a faulty KCNQ1 or KCNH2 gene. Here half the repolarizing potassium current is lost. Inward current wins for longer, so the voltage falls later: the action potential lasts about 60 percent longer and the QT interval lengthens. A long QT raises the risk of a dangerous fast heart rhythm, often set off by exercise, a sudden noise or strong emotion.

What changes it

A sodium channel that fails to inactivate

In congenital long QT type 3 the faulty gene, SCN5A, makes the heart's sodium channel. In a few channels the inactivation gate fails to stay shut, so a small late sodium current keeps leaking in all through the plateau. That extra inward current holds the voltage up against the potassium: here the action potential lasts about 40 percent longer. The QT lengthens by the opposite route: more in, not less out.

    How these teaching models work

    The airway: chloride flow is the share of CFTR channels that reach the membrane times how well each gate opens, both as a share of normal. The liquid's depth runs from about a third of normal with no flow to normal with full flow, because sodium and water follow the chloride. Cilia beat only when the liquid is deeper than about half its normal depth, and mucus clearance follows the cilia.

    The heart muscle cell: after its upswing, the voltage moves against the sum of four currents: calcium flowing in, which fades over the plateau; the repolarizing potassium current, which switches on slowly and is scaled by the potassium slider; the inward rectifier that holds the cell at rest; and, with the second slider, a late sodium current from channels that fail to inactivate. The action potential ends when the voltage is 90 percent of the way back to rest. The model's Q-T interval and refractory period are its own times scaled to the cited references (the refractory period ends when the voltage is most of the way back to rest), so they are illustrative.

    The models leave out much: the many CFTR faults and the drugs suited to each, the kidney and heart-rate effects on the QT, and the rhythms a long QT can set off, which are a later lesson's subject.
