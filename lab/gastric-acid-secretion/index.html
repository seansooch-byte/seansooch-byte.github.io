The Lab / Unit 5: The gut and drug handling / Lesson 16

Stomach acid and the parietal cell.

One kind of cell in your stomach lining pumps out acid strong enough to break down a meal, but only when it is called. Watch that cell work, one piece at a time, then see four kinds of acid-reducing drugs turn it down, each at a different step.

Educational, not for clinical use

Meet the cell

The stomach and its lining

The stomach is a stretchy, muscular bag that churns swallowed food. Its inner layer, the lining, is dotted with tiny openings called gastric pits (gastric means “of the stomach”). Each pit leads down into gastric glands, deeper tubes that make the stomach’s juices.

Meet the cell

The parietal cell

The glands of the stomach’s domed top and main body hold large parietal cells. One face of each looks into the hollow inside of the stomach. The opposite face looks onto the blood side, where tiny blood vessels bring supplies and messages.

Meet the cell

Its job: making acid

A parietal cell makes acid. A liquid is acidic when it holds many hydrogen ions: tiny electrically charged bits of hydrogen. The more hydrogen ions in the stomach, the more acidic it is. The meter tracks how acidic the stomach is.

Making acid

The proton pump

In the stomach-facing surface sit proton pumps: tiny machines that push hydrogen ions (also called protons) out into the stomach and pull potassium, another charged mineral, back in, one for one. Each swap uses energy, because the stomach side already holds far more hydrogen ions than the cell.

Making acid

Chloride joins: hydrochloric acid

Chloride, the charged part of table salt, comes in from the blood side and leaves through a channel, a pore in the stomach-facing surface. Hydrogen ions and chloride together make hydrochloric acid, the stomach’s acid. A second meter tracks how much acid the cell makes.

Making acid

What the acid is for

Acid unravels the proteins in food and switches on pepsin, an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a chemical change) that cuts proteins into pieces. Neighbouring chief cells release it as pepsinogen, an idle form, and the acid turns that into pepsin. The acid also kills most germs swallowed with food.

Making acid

Pumps wait in storage

Between meals most pumps rest inside the cell, packed in tiny bubbles called vesicles. When the cell is called to work, the vesicles merge with the stomach-facing surface and their pumps start. When the call stops, the pumps are pulled back in.

Calling the cell

Histamine calls the pumps up

A neighbouring cell releases histamine, a messenger. It docks on a receptor, a protein on the cell surface shaped to fit one messenger: the H2 receptor (histamine type-two receptor). Docked histamine sends an on signal, and stored pumps rise to the surface.

Calling the cell

Two more messengers add their push

Gastrin, a hormone (a messenger carried in the blood) from the lower stomach, and acetylcholine, released by nerves when you see, smell or eat food (Lesson 6), dock on their own receptors. Gastrin also makes the neighbouring cell release more histamine, and histamine strengthens the push of both.

Guarding the lining

A shield for the lining

Surface cells coat the lining in mucus, a thick slippery gel, laced with bicarbonate, a base: a substance that soaks up hydrogen ions and so cancels acid. Where this shield fails, acid eats a sore into the lining: an ulcer.

Guarding the lining

Prostaglandins: a brake and a shield

The lining also makes prostaglandins, local messengers that put a brake on the parietal cell’s on signal and tell the surface cells to make more mucus and bicarbonate. Pain relievers such as aspirin lower prostaglandins, which is one reason they can cause ulcers (Lesson 18).

Guarding the lining

The whole cell: the key measures

Messengers call pumps to the surface, the pumps make hydrochloric acid, prostaglandins brake the cell and the mucus shield guards the lining. Two measures sum it up: how much acid the cell makes, and how acidic the stomach is. Drugs can lower either one.

What changes it

Proton pump inhibitors: the pumps locked

Proton pump inhibitors, such as omeprazole, lock working pumps shut for good. The messengers still call, but locked pumps cannot push hydrogen ions out, so the acid made and the stomach’s acidity fall, until the cell builds new pumps.

What changes it

H2 receptor blockers: histamine shut out

H2 receptor blockers, such as famotidine, sit in histamine’s receptor without switching it on. Histamine’s push is lost, and so is its boost to gastrin and acetylcholine, so fewer pumps rise: less acid is made and the stomach is less acidic.

What changes it

Antacids: acid cancelled in the stomach

Antacids, such as calcium carbonate, are bases you swallow. They cancel acid already in the stomach, so it turns less acidic quickly, but only for a while: the cell, its messengers and its pumps carry on making acid.

What changes it

Prostaglandin analogues: brake and shield boosted

Prostaglandin analogues, such as misoprostol, are lab-made copies of the lining’s prostaglandins. They press harder on the brake and thicken the mucus and bicarbonate shield: somewhat less acid, and a better-guarded lining, which helps protect people who need NSAIDs. Misoprostol also makes the womb contract, so it must not be used for this in pregnancy; doctors use that effect on purpose in some births.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is worked out relative to the normal cell and settles at once; there is no time course. The on signal is the push of the three messengers, with histamine counted twice over: it pushes on its own (four tenths of the total) and multiplies the push of gastrin and acetylcholine (three tenths each), so losing histamine also weakens the other two. Prostaglandins divide the on signal: the more prostaglandin, the stronger the brake. In the normal cell four in ten pumps are at the surface; the share rises in proportion to the on signal until every pump is up.

    Acid made is the number of working surface pumps compared with normal. A proton pump inhibitor locks a share of the surface pumps; an H2 receptor blocker plugs a share of histamine’s receptors; a prostaglandin analogue adds to the lining’s own prostaglandin, which also thickens the mucus and bicarbonate shield. An antacid touches none of this: it neutralises a share of the acid already in the stomach, so acid made stays the same while the acid left in the stomach falls. How acidic the stomach is follows the acid left on a ten-fold (logarithmic) scale, as the pH scale does. The sizes of every effect are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.

    Left out: food’s own buffering, the timing of meals, somatostatin and other brakes, intrinsic factor, how drugs are absorbed and cleared, and every real-world difference between people. Nothing here is a prediction for a real person, and nothing here is a treatment plan.
