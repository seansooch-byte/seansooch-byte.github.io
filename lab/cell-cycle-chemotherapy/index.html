The Lab / Unit 6: Inflammation, infection, immunity and cancer / Lesson 21

The cell cycle and chemotherapy.

Every new cell in your body comes from an old one splitting in two. Follow one cell round the loop it takes to copy itself, see how cancer breaks that loop, then watch five kinds of chemotherapy strike, each at its own point.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

A cell makes two

Your body is built from cells, tiny living units. To grow, heal and replace worn-out parts, a cell divides: it splits into two new cells, each a full copy of the first. Skin, blood and gut lining are renewed this way every day.

Build the idea

The instructions inside

Inside a cell sits the nucleus, its control room. It holds DNA, the long coded instructions for building and running the cell, wound into bundles called chromosomes. Before a cell divides, it must copy every chromosome, so each new cell gets a full set.

Build the idea

A loop of stages

The cell cycle is the loop a cell goes round to divide. It has four phases, or stages, always in the same order: grow, copy the DNA, check, then split. One trip round makes two cells, and each can start again.

Build the idea

G1: grow and get ready

The first phase is G1 (first gap): the new cell grows, makes what it needs and checks its surroundings. At the end of G1 sits a checkpoint, a gate where the cell decides whether to go on and divide.

Build the idea

The engines that open the gate

The gate is opened by CDKs (cyclin-dependent kinases), enzymes switched on by partner proteins called cyclins. With cyclin D, CDK4 and CDK6 switch off a brake protein, Rb, that holds the cell in G1, so it can go on to S phase.

Build the idea

S phase: copying the DNA

In S phase (synthesis, meaning making), the cell copies all of its DNA. The two strands unzip, and the cell adds matching DNA building blocks, one by one, along each strand, so every chromosome ends up as two joined copies: twice the DNA, the same number of chromosomes.

Build the idea

Untwisting as it copies

DNA is twisted, like a rope, and unzipping it to copy winds the rest tighter. Topoisomerases are enzymes that cut the DNA, let it untwist and join it up again, so copying, and later splitting, can carry on.

Build the idea

G2: a final check

In G2 (second gap), the cell grows a little more and checks the copied DNA for damage. If it finds breaks it cannot fix, a healthy cell stops here, or destroys itself rather than pass on faulty instructions.

Build the idea

M phase: pulling the copies apart

M phase is the split, in two parts. First mitosis: the doubled chromosomes line up, and a spindle of thin protein ropes, microtubules, grabs them, and its ropes shorten to pull one copy to each end while the two ends move apart. Then cytokinesis: the cell pinches in two.

Build the idea

Most cells are resting

Most cells in your body are not dividing. They have stepped out of the loop into G0, a resting state, and simply do their jobs. Some can step back in when needed, for example to heal a wound.

Build the idea

The whole loop: the key measures

Put together: cells grow, copy, check and split, or rest. Some healthy tissues divide all the time, like the bone marrow, the soft centre of bones that makes blood cells, including the white blood cells of Lesson 20. Key measures: how big a tumour grows, and how many new blood cells are made.

What changes it

Cancer: the brakes fail

Cancer is cells dividing out of control. Changes in the DNA jam the engines on or break the brakes at the checkpoint, so fewer cells rest and a tumour, a growing lump of extra cells, builds up. Chemotherapy means drugs that kill dividing cells or stop them dividing.

What changes it

Antimetabolites: fake building blocks in S phase

Antimetabolites, such as fluorouracil and methotrexate, are look-alike DNA building blocks, or drugs that stop the cell making real ones. Cells in S phase, copying their DNA, are damaged and die; cells in other phases are spared, for now. Methotrexate blocks dihydrofolate reductase, the enzyme trimethoprim blocks in bacteria (Lesson 19), but in your own cells.

What changes it

Vinca alkaloids and taxanes: the spindle jammed in M phase

Both jam the spindle. Vinca alkaloids, such as vincristine, stop its microtubule ropes from growing; taxanes, such as paclitaxel, freeze them so they cannot shrink and pull. Either way the chromosomes are not pulled apart: cells get stuck in mitosis and die.

What changes it

Alkylating agents: DNA damaged, in any phase

Alkylating agents, such as cyclophosphamide, stick chemical groups onto DNA, damaging it so it cannot be copied properly. Some, with two sticky ends, also tie the two strands together. They are not phase specific: they work in any phase, even in resting cells, but dividing cells suffer most.

What changes it

Topoisomerase inhibitors: the cut never rejoined

Topoisomerase inhibitors, such as irinotecan and etoposide, trap the untwisting enzyme after it has cut the DNA, so the cut is never joined again. Breaks pile up while the cell copies and checks its DNA, in S phase and G2, and the cell dies.

What changes it

CDK4/6 inhibitors: the G1 gate held shut

CDK4/6 inhibitors, such as palbociclib, are targeted drugs, aimed at two engines rather than all dividing cells. They block CDK4 and CDK6, so the brake stays on and the G1 gate stays shut: cancer cells that rely on them mostly wait in G1, so the tumour grows more slowly; some may stop for good or die.

      How this teaching model works

      Cells sit in five boxes: resting (G0), G1, S, G2 and M. Each phase is left at a steady rate set by how long it lasts, G1 longest and M shortest; a cell leaving M becomes two cells in G1. Some G1 cells step out to rest and some resting cells step back in. The run starts from the mix of phases an untreated population settles into, and the tumour and the bone marrow use the same loop with different resting habits. The tumour speed control speeds up or slows down every phase of the tumour only.

      Each drug class either blocks the way out of a phase or kills cells while they sit in a phase. An antimetabolite slows S phase and kills cells in it. A vinca alkaloid or taxane holds cells in M and kills them there. An alkylating agent damages DNA in every phase and kills a few cells in each, resting ones least; the model does not separate one-ended agents, which only add chemical groups, from two-ended ones, which also tie the strands together. A topoisomerase inhibitor kills cells in S and G2. A CDK4/6 inhibitor blocks most of the way out of G1 and kills none, and acts less strongly on the marrow in this model. Drugs combine: their kills add up and their blocks stack. Tumour size is compared with the untreated tumour of the same speed at the end of the run; new blood cells are all the divisions the marrow made over the run, compared with untreated.

      Left out: how drugs are absorbed and cleared (Lesson 17), DNA repair, resistance, the body’s own control of blood cell making, side effects other than the marrow, and every difference between cancers and between people. Nothing here predicts a real tumour, and nothing here is a treatment plan.
