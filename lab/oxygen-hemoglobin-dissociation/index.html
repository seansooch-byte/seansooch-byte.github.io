The Lab / Unit 3: Breath and blood / Lesson 9

Oxygen and
hemoglobin.

Your blood picks up oxygen in the lungs and hands it over in the tissues, using the same molecule for both jobs. See how one S-shaped curve makes that possible, then watch what slides it, and what robs it.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the curve

Blood carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues

Most cells need oxygen to produce energy. Blood picks it up in the lungs, where air meets blood, and drops it off in the tissues, the working body parts such as muscle and brain. Red blood cells do the carrying.

Build the curve

Each red cell is packed with hemoglobin

Each red blood cell is stuffed with hemoglobin, a protein that holds oxygen. One hemoglobin molecule has four oxygen seats: spots where one oxygen molecule can sit. Oxygen sits loosely, so it can get on and off again.

Build the curve

Oxygen pressure: how hard oxygen pushes

Oxygen pressure measures the oxygen dissolved in the blood itself, not the oxygen held by hemoglobin: it is how hard oxygen presses to move on. It is high in the lungs, where fresh air arrives, and low in working tissues, which use oxygen up. Oxygen always moves from high pressure to low.

Build the curve

More pressure, more seats filled

The higher the oxygen pressure around hemoglobin, the more of its seats fill. The share of seats holding oxygen is called saturation: empty at no pressure, almost completely full at high pressure.

Build the curve

The first oxygen makes the rest easier

Binding means an oxygen sticking to a seat. The first oxygen is the hardest to bind. Once it sits, the molecule changes shape and its other seats bind more easily. This teamwork is cooperative binding. Letting go works the same way in reverse.

Build the curve

Teamwork bends the line into an S

Plot saturation against oxygen pressure and you get the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve, dissociation meaning letting go. Teamwork gives it an S shape: a slow start, a steep middle where small pressure changes move lots of oxygen, and a flat top.

Build the curve

In the lungs, the flat top keeps blood full

Blood leaving the lungs sits on the plateau, the curve’s flat top: almost every seat is full. Because the top is flat, even if lung oxygen pressure dips a little, hemoglobin still loads nearly fully. That is a built-in safety margin.

Build the curve

In the tissues, the steep slope lets go

Working tissues have a low oxygen pressure, which lands on the curve’s steep slope. There, a small drop in pressure empties many seats, so hemoglobin unloads, or hands over, plenty of oxygen exactly where it is needed.

Build the curve

The drop between them is what the tissues get

The gap in height between the lungs point and the tissues point is the share of hemoglobin’s oxygen handed over. At rest, most of it stays on board: a reserve for exercise, when the heart also pumps more blood (Lesson 3).

Build the curve

Half full: where the curve sits

The half-saturation pressure is the oxygen pressure at which half the seats are full. It marks where the curve sits. A curve moved right, a right shift, needs more pressure to fill, so lets go easily; moved left, a left shift, it holds on tighter.

Build the curve

How much oxygen the blood carries

Saturation says what share of seats are full, not how many seats there are. Oxygen content is the total oxygen the blood carries: almost all of it on hemoglobin, so saturation combined with the amount of hemoglobin, plus a little dissolved. Less hemoglobin lowers the ceiling, even if every seat is full.

Build the curve

The whole picture

Lungs load hemoglobin on the flat top; tissues unload it on the steep slope; the drop between is the share handed over. Two key measures: the half-saturation pressure, which says where the curve sits, and the oxygen content, which says how much the blood carries.

What changes it

Right shift: letting go more easily

Busy tissue makes carbon dioxide, a waste gas, and acid, and it warms up. Separately, over hours to days, red cells make more 2,3-BPG, a chemical that loosens hemoglobin’s grip, when oxygen is short, as at altitude or in anemia. All four shift the curve right: at the same tissue pressure, hemoglobin lets go of more oxygen.

What changes it

Left shift: holding on tighter

Fetal hemoglobin, the kind a baby makes before birth, grips oxygen tighter, so it can pull oxygen from the mother’s blood. Cold and alkalosis (blood less acidic than usual) also shift the curve left: hemoglobin loads easily but lets go less in the tissues.

What changes it

Carbon monoxide: stolen seats

Carbon monoxide, a colourless gas from fires and faulty heaters, sticks to oxygen seats far more tightly than oxygen does. Fewer seats carry oxygen, and the rest grip tighter, a left shift. Blood oxygen pressure stays normal, yet content falls.

What changes it

Anemia: fewer seats in the blood

Anemia means too little hemoglobin in the blood, for example after blood loss. Each remaining seat fills normally, so saturation looks normal, but the ceiling drops and content falls. To get the same oxygen, tissues take a bigger share from each pass, so blood leaves them at a lower oxygen pressure.

    How this teaching model works

    The curve follows the Hill equation, saturation = Pn ÷ (Pn + P50n), where P is oxygen pressure. Its steepness n and its half-saturation pressure P50 are fitted at load time through two textbook points from the Clinical values data file: arterial blood (pressure and saturation) and venous blood (pressure and saturation). That fit lands the model’s P50 a little above the separate textbook P50 shown in Clinical values; textbooks differ slightly, and the model keeps Guyton’s two points exact instead.

    Blood leaves the lungs at the arterial pressure (scaled by the lung slider) and loads to the saturation the curve gives there. Oxygen content is saturation × the hemoglobin present × the oxygen each gram binds (from the data file). The tissues remove a fixed amount of oxygen per pass, set at rest from the textbook utilization coefficient and multiplied by the tissue-use slider; the venous saturation is what is left, and the venous oxygen pressure is read back off the curve. Curve position multiplies P50. Carbon monoxide takes a share of seats out of use and lowers P50 for the seats that remain (by 0.6 × that share, an illustrative choice). So with carbon monoxide the model keeps two saturations apart: functional saturation (oxygen-holding seats as a share of the seats still free for oxygen), on which the curve and P50 are defined, and fractional saturation (oxygen-holding seats as a share of all seats), which the Clinical values rows print. At P50, functional saturation is one half, while fractional saturation is lower. If the tissues would need more oxygen than the blood carries, the model stops at a near-empty floor and says the tissues are short.

    The model leaves out oxygen dissolved directly in plasma, blood flow and the heart, carbon dioxide transport, and all timing. The sizes of the drug and disease changes are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.
