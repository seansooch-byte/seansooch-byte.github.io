Myelin and
conduction velocity.

An impulse creeps along a bare nerve fiber but jumps from gap to gap along one wrapped in myelin. See why the wrapping makes it fast, then watch it slow and finally stop when myelin is lost in the brain and spinal cord, as in multiple sclerosis, or in a peripheral nerve, as in Guillain-Barre syndrome.

Educational, not for clinical use

How an impulse travels

A nerve fiber carries impulses

A neuron is a nerve cell. From its cell body runs the axon, a long, thin cable, also called a nerve fiber. A message travels along it as an impulse, the brief electrical spike called an action potential (Lesson 28). This lesson asks how fast the impulse travels, and what slows or stops it.

How an impulse travels

Charge spreads ahead, and fades

Where the impulse is, sodium rushes into the axon. Its positive charge spreads along the inside, ahead of the impulse: the spreading charge. The curve shows how far it lifts the membrane above its resting voltage. On the way, some charge seeps out through the membrane, a leak, so the farther it spreads, the weaker it gets.

How an impulse travels

The next patch fires only at threshold

A patch of membrane fires its own action potential only if the spreading charge lifts it to threshold, the voltage at which its sodium channels open together (Lesson 28). Close to the impulse the charge is strong enough. A little farther on it has faded below threshold, so the patches there cannot fire yet.

How an impulse travels

A bare axon fires patch by patch

On a bare axon, with no wrapping, the charge fades within a short distance, so it can only lift the patch right next to the one firing. That patch fires, lifts the next, and so on: continuous conduction. Every patch along the whole axon has to fire in turn, and each firing takes time, so the impulse creeps along slowly.

How an impulse travels

Speed: length over time

Conduction velocity is how fast the impulse travels: the length of the fiber divided by the time the impulse takes to cover it. Here a timing bar grows from the moment the impulse starts until it reaches the far end. For the same length, a longer bar means a slower velocity. Bars like this compare fibers from here on.

Myelin

Myelin wraps the axon

Many axons are wrapped in myelin: cell membrane, rich in fat, wound round the axon in many tight layers, like tape round a wire. The wrapping is the myelin sheath; the lens shows its layers in a cut end. Fat carries charge poorly, so the sheath works as insulation, keeping charge in.

Myelin

Two kinds of cells make myelin

In the brain and spinal cord, the central nervous system, myelin comes from the oligodendrocyte: one cell reaches out with several arms, each wrapping a stretch of an axon. In the nerves running out to the body, the peripheral nervous system, each myelin-making Schwann cell wraps one stretch of one axon.

Myelin

Gaps in the sheath: nodes of Ranvier

The sheath comes in segments, with a short bare gap between them: a node of Ranvier. The covered stretch between two nodes is an internode. The axon's voltage-gated sodium channels are packed into the nodes, so on a myelinated axon the nodes are the places an action potential can fire.

Myelin

Myelin keeps the charge in

Under the sheath, very little charge leaks out, so the spreading charge stays strong over a much longer distance. It reaches the next node, a whole internode away, still well above threshold, so that node fires. The dashed curve is the bare axon from step 2: there the charge falls below threshold long before it gets that far.

Where myelin can fail

Central and peripheral nerves

The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system. Inside them, myelinated axons run packed together in the white matter, pale because of myelin's fat. Peripheral nerves leave the spinal cord and run to the muscles and skin of the body; Schwann cells myelinate some of their axons, while others stay bare. A disease can strike myelin in one system and spare the other.

Where myelin can fail

Losing myelin

Demyelination is the loss of myelin from an axon that had it. Its causes vary: some are genetic, some infections, and some an attack on the sheath by the body's own immune system. Where the sheath is thin or gone, charge leaks out again, much as on a bare axon, so less of it reaches the next node. The dashed outlines show the sheath that was there.

What changes it

Saltatory conduction

With myelin, the charge from one node stays strong enough to lift the next node to threshold, so only the nodes fire. The impulse seems to jump from node to node: saltatory conduction, from the Latin for "to leap". Charge travels fast under the sheath and far fewer firings are needed, so the impulse reaches the far end much sooner than on the bare axon, dashed.

What changes it

Multiple sclerosis (central myelin lost)

In multiple sclerosis (MS), the immune system destroys myelin in the brain and spinal cord, leaving scarred patches, plaques (sclerosis means hardening). Here a plaque has stripped about half the myelin from three internodes. Charge leaks there, so each node in the plaque takes longer to reach threshold. The impulse still gets through as a full action potential, but later.

What changes it

Guillain-Barre syndrome (peripheral myelin lost)

In Guillain-Barre syndrome, often after an infection, the immune system attacks peripheral nerves. In its demyelinating form, shown here, it strips their Schwann cell myelin; other forms injure the axon itself. Here half the myelin is gone along five internodes of a nerve to the leg. The impulse slows through that whole stretch, so signals arrive late. Weakness and tingling follow, often rising from the legs.

What changes it

Conduction block and slowed velocity

One patch loses more and more of its myelin. At first the impulse only slows: the charge reaching the next node is weaker, but still lifts it to threshold. In this model, once more than three quarters of the myelin is lost, the charge falls short of threshold and the next node never fires: conduction block. No action potential reaches the far end, so there is no peak; it stays at its resting voltage.

    How this teaching model works

    The fiber has eight internodes between nine nodes. An action potential at one node sends charge spreading to the next. Under healthy myelin that charge lifts the next node 60 mV above rest, an illustrative figure. In the damaged patch the lift shrinks in step with the share of myelin lost: half the myelin lost, half the lift.

    The next node fires if the lift reaches threshold. The resting and threshold voltages come from the Clinical values data file, so the gap between them sets how much lift is needed. With that gap, a patch can lose up to 75% of its myelin and still pass the impulse; more than 75% blocks it at the first node of the patch.

    Each node takes a short fixed time to fire, plus a time to reach threshold that grows as the lift shrinks: a weaker lift takes longer. A bare axon has no sheath and fires patch by patch, so it covers the same length far more slowly. Every node that fires fires the same full action potential. The model leaves out axon width, temperature, how real demyelinated axons can regrow channels or myelin, and the many fibers of a whole nerve.
