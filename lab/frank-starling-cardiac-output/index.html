The Lab / Unit 1: The heart, the kidney and blood pressure / Lesson 3

Frank–Starling
& cardiac output.

Why a fuller heart pumps harder, and how filling, contraction strength and heart rate add up to the blood the heart pumps each minute. Then watch fluids, drugs and heart failure move the heart along its curve, or move the curve itself.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

A pump for the body

A ventricle is one of the heart’s two lower chambers: a bag of muscle that pushes blood out. We follow the left ventricle, which pumps blood to the whole body. With every beat it relaxes and fills, then squeezes and empties, while one-way valves keep the blood moving forward.

Build the idea

First, blood returns

Between beats the ventricle relaxes and fills. Venous return is the blood flowing back to the heart through the veins; it reaches the left ventricle after passing through the lungs. The more blood returns, the fuller the chamber is when the next beat starts.

Build the idea

Filling stretches the muscle

Filling stretches the muscle fibres in the ventricle wall. Preload is that stretch just before the beat. The usual stand-in for it is the end-diastolic volume, the blood in the chamber at the end of filling: diastole is the relaxed, filling part of each beat.

Build the idea

More stretch, more force

Inside each fibre, thin and thick protein filaments overlap, and heads on the thick ones pull. Within the heart’s working range, stretch lines them up better and makes them respond more strongly, so the fibre pulls with more force. This is the Frank–Starling mechanism: more filling, a stronger next beat.

Build the idea

Blood in one beat

Stroke volume is the blood one ventricle pushes out in one beat. A squeeze never empties the chamber: some blood always stays behind. With everything else the same, a fuller chamber gives a bigger stroke volume.

Build the idea

Follow the rising curve

Plot blood per beat against filling and you get the Frank–Starling curve. Read it left to right: more filling, more blood per beat. It is steep at first, then levels off, because past a point extra filling adds little. A resting heart sits on the rising part, with room to pump more.

Build the idea

Strength is a separate input

Contractility is how hard the muscle squeezes from the same stretch. The sympathetic nerves, the body’s stress signals, raise it. A stronger heart pumps more from the same filling, so its whole curve sits higher; a weaker heart’s whole curve sits lower.

Build the idea

Count the beats

Heart rate is how many times the heart beats in a minute. More beats send more blood forward. But the ventricle fills only in the relaxed part of the gap between squeezes, its time to fill, and the faster the heart beats, the more that time shrinks.

Build the idea

The flow over a minute

Cardiac output is the blood one ventricle pumps in a minute: stroke volume times heart rate. A faster heart raises it, up to a point. At very fast rates each beat has so little time to fill that it pumps much less, and output falls.

Build the idea

See the whole picture

Filling and strength set the blood per beat; heart rate sets how many beats; together they make the cardiac output. One more input is held steady in this lesson: afterload, the load the ventricle pushes against as it empties, roughly the pressure in the main artery, which Lesson 1 shows.

What changes it

Intravenous fluids: more blood returns

Intravenous (IV) fluids are given into a vein. They add to the blood returning to the heart, so the ventricle fills more and moves right and up along its curve: more blood per beat, more each minute. A failing heart, on a low flat curve, gains little and can develop congestion: fluid backing up into the lungs and body tissues.

What changes it

Diuretics: less filling

Diuretics help the kidneys remove salt and water (Lesson 2). Less blood returns, filling falls, and a healthy heart moves left and down along its curve: less per beat. A failing heart on a low, flat curve is different: there extra filling mostly adds congestion, so removing it costs little output.

What changes it

Positive inotropes: more strength

Positive inotropes are medicines that raise contractility, such as dobutamine, milrinone and digoxin. Filling stays the same, but the whole curve lifts, so each beat from that same filling pumps more, and so does every minute.

What changes it

Beta-blockers: slower and gentler

Beta-blockers block the stress signals at the heart, so it beats slower and squeezes a little less hard. On its own the gentler squeeze would pump less per beat: the curve dips. But slower beats leave more time to fill, sliding the heart right: in this model blood per beat changes little. Fewer beats pump less blood each minute.

What changes it

Venodilators: room in the veins

Venodilators, such as nitrates, relax the veins so they hold more blood, away from the heart. Less blood returns, filling falls, and the heart moves left and down along its curve: less per beat. Nitrates can also widen arteries, which this picture leaves out.

What changes it

Heart failure: filling is not enough

In heart failure the heart cannot pump enough for the body’s needs. Here the muscle is weak, so the whole curve drops. The kidneys then hold on to salt and water and filling rises, but on the low, flat curve that wins back little per beat while fluid backs up as congestion. Some heart failure keeps a normal squeeze.

    How this teaching model works

    Blood per beat follows a curve that rises with filling and levels off, scaled by contraction strength and kept under 95% of the filled volume, so some blood always stays behind. The graph draws this same curve. Cardiac output is blood per beat times heart rate. Heart rate also changes how long each beat has to fill: below the normal rate filling rises a little, and above it a smooth filling penalty grows, sharply at very fast rates, so output rises and then falls as the rate climbs. The lesson’s beta-blocker slows the heart as much as Lesson 1’s does and softens the squeeze a little; its heart failure first weakens the squeeze, then lets filling rise. This conceptual model leaves out reflexes, blood pressure, afterload, valve disease and long-term changes.
