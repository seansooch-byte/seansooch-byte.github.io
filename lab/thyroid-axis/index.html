The Lab / Core medicine / Lesson 14

The thyroid axis.

A small gland in your neck sets how fast your whole body runs, and your brain keeps it in check with a brake. Build the loop one piece at a time, see the two ways it tips over, then watch four kinds of treatment each act on a different part.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

A small gland in the neck

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland, an organ that makes and releases chemicals. It wraps around the front of the windpipe, just below the voice box. Its job is to set how fast the whole body runs.

Build the idea

Its hormone sets the body’s pace

The gland releases thyroid hormone into the blood. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood. This one reaches almost every cell and sets the metabolic rate: how fast cells burn fuel at rest, and so how much heat they make.

Build the idea

Built from iodine

Thyroid hormone is built from iodine, a mineral we get from food such as iodized salt and seafood. Inside the gland, tiny hollow balls of cells called follicles pull iodine out of the blood and fix it onto a stored protein.

Build the idea

Two forms: T4 and T3

The gland mostly releases T4 (thyroxine), which carries four iodine atoms, plus a little T3, with three. Cells around the body snip one iodine off T4 to make T3, the stronger form that does most of the work.

Build the idea

The pituitary gives the order

The pituitary, a pea-sized gland at the base of the brain, sends TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) to the thyroid. TSH docks on the follicles and tells them to take up iodine and release T4 and T3. More TSH, more hormone.

Build the idea

The hypothalamus starts the chain

Just above sits the hypothalamus, a small brain region that links the nerves to the hormones. It sends TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone) down to the pituitary, telling it to release TSH. The chain runs brain, pituitary, thyroid.

Build the idea

Enough hormone puts on the brake

T4 and T3 in the blood report back to the brain. When they run high, the hypothalamus and pituitary release less TRH and TSH, so the thyroid slows. The result switches off its own cause: negative feedback.

Build the idea

Too little hormone turns the order up

When T4 and T3 run low, the brake eases. The pituitary releases more TSH and pushes the gland harder until levels climb back. So TSH moves the opposite way to thyroid hormone, the key clue for reading the loop.

Build the idea

It also speeds the heart

Thyroid hormone adds docking sites for catecholamines, the body’s adrenaline-like alarm signals (Lesson 6), so the heart and blood vessels answer them more strongly. More hormone: a faster, harder heartbeat. Less: a slower one.

Build the idea

The whole loop: the key measures

Brain, pituitary and thyroid form one loop, held steady by the brake. It is read with thyroid function tests: blood tests of TSH and T4, often T3 too. Because of the brake, TSH usually moves opposite to T4 and T3.

What changes it

Hypothyroidism: an underactive gland

In hypothyroidism the gland makes too little hormone. The usual cause is Hashimoto thyroiditis: the immune system, the body’s defence against germs, attacks the gland. T4 and T3 fall, TSH climbs, and the body slows down and feels cold.

What changes it

Hyperthyroidism: an overactive gland

In hyperthyroidism the gland makes too much. In Graves’ disease, the usual cause, antibodies, immune proteins, latch onto the gland’s TSH docking sites and push it to grow and pour out hormone. The brake drives TSH down; the heart races.

What changes it

Levothyroxine: the missing hormone as a tablet

Levothyroxine is a lab-made hormone identical to the body’s own T4, taken as a tablet for hypothyroidism. It tops up what the damaged gland cannot make. Cells turn it into T3 as usual, and the brake brings TSH back down.

What changes it

Thionamides: the gland builds less

Thionamides, such as methimazole and propylthiouracil, are tablets that enter the gland and stop it fixing iodine onto its stored protein and joining the pieces into hormone. Less T4 and T3 is made, the brake eases and TSH rises back.

What changes it

Radioactive iodine: shrinking the gland

The thyroid pulls iodine from the blood, so radioactive iodine, a form that gives off damaging radiation, collects there and destroys overactive tissue. The gland shrinks, hormone falls and TSH rises back. Destroy too much and the gland becomes underactive.

What changes it

Beta-blockers: calming the heart only

Beta-blockers block the heart’s docking sites for alarm signals, so a racing heartbeat slows. They do not touch the gland: T4, T3 and TSH stay where the disease put them, so they ease symptoms while another treatment fixes the gland.

    How this teaching model works

    Every level is worked out relative to the normal body and settles to a steady state; there is no time course. The pituitary’s TSH falls steeply as T4 in the blood rises (in the model, in proportion to T4 raised to the power −2.2), which is the negative-feedback brake; TRH falls with T4 too. The gland’s release is its working tissue × the share of its hormone-building it can still do × its iodine supply × the drive from TSH, which saturates at twice normal, plus any push from antibodies acting like TSH. T4 in the blood is the gland’s release plus any T4 from a tablet; T3 is made mostly from T4 in body cells and a little by the gland. The loop settles where these agree, found by halving the search range seventy times.

    The body’s pace follows T3, and the heartbeat follows the pace; a beta-blocker blunts the heartbeat only. Gland size grows with working tissue, with antibody push and with a high TSH, which is how a goiter (a swollen gland) forms when iodine runs short. Each treatment moves the model in the direction of its cited mechanism: levothyroxine adds T4 from outside the gland, a thionamide blocks most of the gland’s hormone-building, radioactive iodine destroys about three quarters of its tissue, and a beta-blocker leaves the loop alone. The sizes of all these effects are illustrative choices; only their directions are meant to teach.

    The model never turns its levels into lab numbers: the sources give only the direction of each change, so the readout says where each level sits against its healthy band, and the chart has no numbers at all; its shaded box is where TSH and T4 are both inside their healthy bands (the model’s normal sits in the middle of each band; for TSH, the middle on a ratio scale). The real reference numbers are in the Clinical values tab. Left out: binding proteins and free hormone, the weeks the loop takes to settle, pregnancy, illness, other medicines and every real-world difference between people. Nothing here is a prediction for a real person.
