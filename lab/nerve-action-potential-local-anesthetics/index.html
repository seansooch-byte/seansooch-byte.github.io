Nerve action potential
and local anesthetics.

A nerve impulse is a rush of sodium through voltage-gated channels, passed from one patch of membrane to the next. Watch it fire and travel, then see a local anesthetic slip inside the nerve, plug those channels and stop the impulse before it reaches the brain.

Educational, not for clinical use

The resting nerve

A nerve cell sends signals down its axon

A nerve cell, or neuron, carries messages. Its cell body holds the nucleus. From it runs the axon, a long, thin cable; one can stretch from a fingertip toward the spinal cord. A message travels along the axon as an electrical impulse, and this lesson follows one from start to finish.

The resting nerve

At rest, the inside is negative

Up close, the axon is wrapped in a thin, oily membrane. A slight excess of negative charge lines its inside face, and of positive charge its outside face. That gives a voltage across it, the membrane potential, measured in millivolts (mV), inside compared with outside. At rest it sits near mV: the resting potential.

The resting nerve

Sodium waits outside

Sodium is an ion, a particle with a positive charge, and it is far more crowded outside the axon than inside. Two things urge it in: crowding, the concentration gradient, pushes it from crowded to sparse, and the negative inside pulls on its positive charge, the electrical pull. But the membrane keeps it out until a door for sodium opens.

The resting nerve

The sodium channel has two gates

That door is the voltage-gated sodium channel, a protein that opens when the voltage changes. It has two gates. At rest its activation gate is shut and the channel is ready. When the inside turns less negative, that gate opens and sodium pours in. A moment later the inactivation gate swings shut under it: the channel is inactivated until the voltage returns toward rest.

The action potential

Threshold: the point of no return

A stimulus, such as a touch on the skin, lets a little sodium in and makes the inside less negative: depolarization. A small nudge fades away. But at mV, the threshold, enough voltage-gated sodium channels open that the inrush feeds itself: each channel that opens lets in sodium that opens more, and the voltage shoots up.

The action potential

The sodium rush

Past threshold, sodium channels open together and sodium rushes in. The inside swings positive, up to about mV. This brief spike in voltage is the action potential, the nerve impulse itself. Its quick rise, the upstroke, is carried by sodium coming in through the open channels.

The action potential

Back down: inactivation and potassium

At the peak, the inactivation gates shut, so no more sodium enters. Voltage-gated potassium channels open, and potassium, more crowded inside, flows out, taking positive charge with it. The voltage falls back toward rest, repolarization, and briefly dips below it, hyperpolarization, because the potassium channels are slow to close.

The action potential

All or none

An action potential fires fully or not at all: it is all or none. A stimulus too weak to reach threshold fires nothing. Any stimulus that reaches threshold fires one that peaks at the same voltage, however strong the stimulus. A stronger stimulus never makes a bigger action potential; it can only fire them more often.

The action potential

Refractory period: absolute, then relative

Just after firing, the fiber cannot fire as usual: the refractory period. First comes the absolute refractory period: the inactivation gates are shut, so no stimulus, however strong, starts another action potential. Then comes the relative refractory period: the gates have reset, but potassium is still flowing out, so only a stronger stimulus than usual can start one.

The action potential

The impulse travels along the axon

Sodium entering one patch of membrane spreads along the inside and lifts the next patch to threshold, so it fires too, and so on down the axon: propagation. The patch that just fired is refractory, so the impulse cannot turn back. Every patch fires the same full action potential, so the impulse arrives at the far end as strong as it started.

How the drug gets in

Where a local anesthetic works

A local anesthetic is a drug injected near a nerve, as a dentist does, to numb one area while the person stays awake. Pain starts as impulses in nerve fibers from the skin, which travel to the spinal cord and brain. Stop them on the way, and the brain never hears about the pain. Here the drug has just been injected and impulses still pass.

How the drug gets in

Uncharged to get in, charged to block

The drug trades a hydrogen ion (the particle that makes a fluid acidic) back and forth, so it comes in two forms. Its uncharged form dissolves through the oily membrane; its charged form, holding a hydrogen ion, cannot cross. Inside, uncharged drug picks one up, and the charged form enters the sodium channel from the inside and plugs it at its binding site.

What changes it

Voltage-gated sodium channel block

Plugged channels cannot let sodium in. Here about seven in ten channels along the drug's stretch are blocked. Too little sodium enters there for the spreading charge to lift the next patch to threshold, so the impulse stops. Beyond it no action potential fires at all: the far end stays at rest and the brain gets no pain signal. In this model, impulses fail once more than half the channels are blocked.

What changes it

Use-dependent block of open and inactivated channels

The drug reaches its site most easily while the channel is open or inactivated, not while it is ready and shut. So block builds with use. A slowly firing fiber stays lightly blocked and its impulses pass. A fast-firing one, as pain fibers often are, gains block with every impulse: here the first two get through, then the block passes this model's cutoff of half the channels and the rest fail.

What changes it

Acidic tissue

Infected or inflamed tissue is acidic: it holds extra hydrogen ions. More of the drug picks one up and stays charged outside the nerve, where the charged form cannot cross. Less drug gets inside, so far fewer channels are blocked, here about two in ten instead of seven, and the impulse gets through. That is why a local anesthetic often numbs an infected area poorly.

What changes it

Amide and ester local anesthetics

Local anesthetics come in two chemical families, named for the link in the middle of the molecule. Esters and amides plug the sodium channel the same way; they differ in how the body clears them once they wash into the blood. Typically, plasma enzymes called esterases break down esters, while the liver mainly breaks down amides. Articaine is the exception: an amide with an ester group, largely broken down in plasma.

    How this teaching model works

    The resting potential, the threshold and the peak are the cited typical values from the Clinical values data file. The stimulus lifts the first patch of membrane part of the way to threshold, or to it and beyond; at or above threshold an action potential fires, and it always peaks at the same voltage.

    Each patch that fires lets sodium in, and that charge spreading inside lifts the next patch by a fixed amount when every sodium channel is free. Along the drug's stretch, that lift shrinks with the share of channels still free, so once more than half are blocked the next patch never reaches threshold and the impulse stops there.

    Outside the nerve, a share of the drug is uncharged; only that share crosses the membrane. Acid outside keeps more of it charged, so less gets in. Inside, the drug plugs channels, and faster firing lets it bind more (use-dependent block). The family changes only where the body breaks the drug down, not how it blocks.

    Every number but the three voltages is an illustrative teaching choice, not a measured value or an amount anyone could be given. The model leaves out myelin, how a partly blocked patch fires a smaller and slower rise, how long the drug lasts, and the drug's effects elsewhere in the body.
