The Lab / Core medicine / Lesson 10

Air meets
blood.

Every breath only matters if blood is there to meet it. Build up how air and blood share each tiny air sac in the lung, then watch what extra oxygen, airway drugs, a clot and an infection do to the match.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

Air and blood meet in the lungs

Each breath carries air down branching tubes, the airways, to millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli (one is an alveolus). Blood pumped from the heart flows past them. This is where your body picks up oxygen.

Build the idea

Each air sac is wrapped in tiny blood vessels

Each alveolus is wrapped in a mesh of capillaries: blood vessels so narrow that red blood cells pass in single file. Only an extremely thin wall separates the air from the blood, so gases cross it easily.

Build the idea

Oxygen goes in, carbon dioxide comes out

Gas exchange happens across that wall. Oxygen moves from the air into the blood. Carbon dioxide, the waste gas cells make, moves from the blood into the air to be breathed out. Each gas moves from more to less.

Build the idea

Ventilation: fresh air in, used air out

Ventilation is the flow of air in and out of the alveoli. Each breath brings in oxygen-rich air and carries carbon dioxide away. The more air a sac gets, the closer its gas stays to fresh air.

Build the idea

Perfusion: blood flowing past

Perfusion is the flow of blood past the alveoli. Blood arrives from the body low in oxygen and high in carbon dioxide, and leaves topped up with oxygen. The more blood flows past a sac, the more oxygen it takes from that sac.

Build the idea

Air and blood need to match

Each alveolus works best when its air and its blood are in balance. The ventilation–perfusion ratio compares the two: air delivered against blood flowing past. When they balance, the match is good and blood leaves full of oxygen.

Build the idea

Too little air: blood leaves short of oxygen

If a sac gets too little air for its blood, its oxygen runs low and blood leaves only partly loaded. With no air at all, blood passes through unchanged: a shunt, blood that crosses the lungs without meeting air.

Build the idea

Too little blood: air is wasted

If a sac gets plenty of air but little blood, most of that air is not used. With no blood at all, air goes in and out for nothing: dead space, air that reaches the lungs but never meets blood.

Build the idea

Blood from every sac mixes

Blood from every alveolus mixes into arterial blood, which the heart pumps to the body. Well-aired sacs cannot make up for a poorly aired one: their blood is already nearly full of oxygen (Lesson 9). So a little oxygen-poor blood drags the whole mix down.

Build the idea

The lung steers blood toward air

When an alveolus runs low on oxygen, the small blood vessels around it tighten. This hypoxic vasoconstriction, meaning vessels narrowing where oxygen is low, steers blood toward better-aired sacs, so the mismatch shrinks.

Build the idea

The whole picture

Ventilation brings air, perfusion brings blood, and each alveolus needs the two in balance. Two key measures: the ventilation–perfusion ratio, which says how well air and blood match, and the oxygen in arterial blood, the result of every sac mixed together.

What changes it

Supplemental oxygen: richer air

Supplemental oxygen means breathing air with extra oxygen added, through a mask or soft nasal tubes. Every ventilated sac fills with more oxygen, so arterial blood carries more. It cannot reach blood in a shunt, because that blood never meets the air.

What changes it

Beta-2 agonists: switching on the relax signal

In asthma, the muscle around the airways tightens, so some sacs get too little air. Bronchodilators widen airways. Beta-2 agonists, one kind, switch on the muscle’s relaxing signal (Lesson 6), so the airway opens and air reaches the sacs again.

What changes it

Muscarinic antagonists: blocking the tighten signal

Nerves tighten airway muscle by releasing a messenger that lands on muscarinic receptors, the muscle’s receiving sites for it. Muscarinic antagonists, the other kind of bronchodilator, block those receptors, so the tightening signal cannot land. The muscle eases and air returns to the sacs.

What changes it

Pulmonary embolism: blood flow blocked

A pulmonary embolism is a blood clot, often from a leg vein, stuck in a lung artery. Sacs beyond it keep their air but lose their blood: dead space. Their blood is pushed to other sacs, which now get too little air, so oxygen falls.

What changes it

Pneumonia: air sacs fill with fluid

Pneumonia is a lung infection. Infected alveoli fill with fluid and pus, so no air reaches them, yet blood still flows past: a shunt. That blood leaves without fresh oxygen, and extra oxygen in the air cannot reach it.

    How this teaching model works

    Two air sacs share the blood that leaves the heart, after a small bypass that skips them both. Each sac normally gets half the air and half the remaining blood. Its ventilation–perfusion ratio is scaled so that a normal sac has the whole-lung ratio from the Clinical values data file. For each sac, alveolar carbon dioxide follows a simple balance, PACO2 = PvCO2 ÷ (1 + k × ratio), and alveolar oxygen is solved by bisection from the oxygen balance k′ × ratio × (PIO2 − PAO2) = content(PAO2) − content(venous). The two constants k and k′ are set so that a normal sac reproduces OpenStax’s alveolar gas exactly, so a sac with no air holds the gas of blood arriving from the body and a sac with no blood holds the air breathed in.

    Oxygen content here is hemoglobin saturation only, on a Hill-shaped curve. Its steepness is an illustrative choice of the model; its position is set so that blood at OpenStax’s pulmonary-vein oxygen pressure sits at the middle of OpenStax’s saturation range. Oxygen dissolved directly in the blood is left out, so with supplemental oxygen the model understates how far the oxygen pressure of blood leaving the lungs rises. The bypass is sized at load time so that the healthy model’s mixed blood lands exactly on OpenStax’s pulmonary-vein value; it stands in for the small mismatches of a real lung and is not a reference value. Blood leaving each sac is fully equilibrated with that sac’s gas, and carbon dioxide content is treated as proportional to its pressure.

    A clot sends blood away from the right sac to the left one, so total blood flow stays the same. Fluid fills part of the right sac, taking away its air and turning the blood past it into shunt. With hypoxic vasoconstriction switched on, blood flow to the right sac shrinks toward three tenths of its set value as that sac’s oxygen falls toward the level of arriving blood (an illustrative rule, found by repeated adjustment until it settles).

    Left out: breathing harder to compensate, the heart and the tissues (blood always arrives with the same gases), diffusion limits, gravity and the many sacs of a real lung. The sizes of the drug and disease changes are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.
