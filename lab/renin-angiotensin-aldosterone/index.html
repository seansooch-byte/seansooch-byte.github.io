The Lab / Unit 1: The heart, the kidney and blood pressure / Lesson 5

Renin, angiotensin
& aldosterone.

Three chemical messengers passed along like a relay: the kidney’s way of raising blood pressure and holding on to salt. Build the chain one link at a time, then see where four kinds of drugs cut it, and why the links before each cut rise.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the chain

The kidneys keep watch on pressure

Your two kidneys clean the blood and make urine. They also feel the blood pressure, the push of blood against artery walls, in the tiny vessels that feed each of their filters: sensor cells sit in those vessel walls, just before the filter. Lesson 4 covers pressure itself.

Build the chain

Low pressure releases renin

When the pressure they feel falls, when nearby tube cells sense less salt flowing past and signal them, or when sympathetic nerves signal them, the sensor cells release more renin into the blood. Renin is an enzyme: a protein that speeds up one chemical reaction, here cutting another protein.

Build the chain

Renin cuts angiotensinogen

The liver keeps angiotensinogen, a long protein, in the blood all the time. Renin snips a short piece off its end. That piece is angiotensin I (“angiotensin one”), which does very little by itself. More renin means more angiotensin I.

Build the chain

An enzyme in the lungs makes the active messenger

In the lungs, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE for short), on the inner lining of the vessels, snips the last two building blocks off angiotensin I. The result is angiotensin II (“angiotensin two”), a hormone: a chemical messenger carried in the blood that changes what distant organs do.

Build the chain

A messenger needs a receptor

Angiotensin II only works where it can dock. A receptor is a cell’s docking site for one particular messenger, shaped to fit it like a lock fits a key. When angiotensin II docks, the cell answers. Cells without the receptor ignore it.

Build the chain

It squeezes the smallest arteries

Arterioles are the smallest arteries, wrapped in muscle. Angiotensin II docks on their receptors and the muscle tightens, so they narrow. Blood finds it harder to get through, called higher resistance, and the pressure behind it rises within minutes.

Build the chain

It calls out aldosterone

Angiotensin II also reaches the adrenal glands, two small glands sitting on top of the kidneys. Docking on their receptors makes them release aldosterone, the third messenger in the relay. A high blood potassium calls it out too. Aldosterone travels in the blood back to the kidneys.

Build the chain

Aldosterone holds on to salt

Each kidney makes urine in tiny tubes called nephrons. Near their end, aldosterone makes the tube cells take more sodium, the main part of table salt, back into the blood, and send more potassium, another mineral carried in the blood, out in the urine. Lesson 2 follows the nephron.

Build the chain

Water follows the salt

Where sodium goes, water follows. Keeping salt keeps water, so blood volume, the amount of blood in the circulation, grows. A fuller circulation pushes harder on artery walls, so pressure rises. This is the slow arm: it works over hours to days.

Build the chain

The chain switches itself off

Here is the whole chain as one relay. As pressure climbs back, the kidney feels the push and releases less renin, so each later link makes less. This is negative feedback: a result that turns down its own cause. It holds pressure steady even when you eat more or less salt.

Build the chain

The whole chain

Low pressure releases renin. Renin cuts angiotensinogen into angiotensin I; ACE makes angiotensin II. It narrows arterioles and calls out aldosterone, which keeps salt and water. Pressure rises, and feedback eases renin. Three measures to watch: blood pressure, blood volume and potassium.

What changes it

ACE inhibitors: no conversion

An inhibitor is a drug that stops an enzyme working. ACE inhibitors stop ACE, so little angiotensin II is made: arterioles relax, aldosterone falls and pressure drops. The kidney answers with more renin, so angiotensin I piles up unused. Potassium rises. ACE also breaks down bradykinin, so bradykinin builds up, which can cause a dry cough.

What changes it

Angiotensin receptor blockers: the main dock is taken

Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) block the receptor angiotensin II uses to squeeze arterioles and call out aldosterone. Arterioles relax, aldosterone falls and pressure drops. The kidney releases more renin, so the blood holds more angiotensin II, but it cannot use that receptor. Potassium rises. ACE still works, so bradykinin does not build up.

What changes it

Aldosterone antagonists: salt lets go

An antagonist blocks a receptor without switching it on. Aldosterone antagonists, such as spironolactone, block aldosterone’s receptor in the nephron. Less sodium and water are kept, so volume and pressure fall, and potassium builds up. The kidney answers the fall with more renin, and the body makes more aldosterone, to little effect.

What changes it

Direct renin inhibitors: the first link cut

Direct renin inhibitors stop renin itself, so far less angiotensinogen is cut. Every later link falls: less angiotensin I, less angiotensin II, less aldosterone, and pressure drops. The kidney releases even more renin, but the drug keeps it from working. Potassium rises.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is relative to a normal resting state, where every quantity equals 1. The kidney releases renin; with feedback working, the amount rises steeply when pressure is below normal and falls when it is above. Renin’s working share (set by the renin slider) cuts angiotensinogen, which the model treats as never running out, so angiotensin I follows working renin. ACE’s working share turns angiotensin I into angiotensin II, and the free share of angiotensin II receptors sets the signal reaching cells. That signal tightens arterioles (resistance) and, with a resting floor, sets aldosterone release; an adrenal tumor can add aldosterone on top. Aldosterone’s free receptors set its effect. Salt kept is the average of aldosterone’s effect and the angiotensin II signal (angiotensin II also helps the kidney keep sodium directly). Blood volume rises gently with salt kept and with salt eaten, and pressure rises with the square of volume times resistance. Potassium falls as aldosterone’s effect rises, and sodium moves only very slightly, because water follows the salt. The model finds the steady state where the pressure the kidney senses equals the pressure the chain produces. It omits thirst, the brain’s water-holding hormone, nerves, kidney filtering, potassium’s own pull on aldosterone, and anything about timing. In the Clinical values tab the relative results are multiplied by the textbook typical values to show illustrative numbers. The bars in the pictures stretch big changes and small ones differently so both stay readable; only their direction is meant to teach.
