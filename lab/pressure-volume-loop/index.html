Pressure–Volume Loop

How the heart's main pumping chamber fills and empties with every beat, drawn as one simple picture, one step at a time.

How the loop works

Meet the left ventricle

The left ventricle is the heart's main pumping chamber: a muscle bag that fills with blood, then squeezes it out to the body. One-way doors called valves keep blood moving forward: the mitral valve lets blood in, the aortic valve lets it out.

How the loop works

Two things to watch: volume and pressure

We follow the ventricle with two measures. Volume is how much blood is inside it. Pressure is how hard that blood is being pushed. Each moment of a heartbeat is one dot: further right means fuller, higher up means squeezing harder.

How the loop works · phase 1 of 4

Filling: the bottom edge

Phase 1, filling. The ventricle relaxes and the mitral valve opens, so blood flows in. Volume grows a lot while pressure stays low, so the dot slides to the right along the bottom. This is the loop's bottom edge.

How the loop works · phase 2 of 4

Squeezing with both valves shut: the right edge

Phase 2, isovolumic contraction (a "same-volume squeeze"). The muscle starts to squeeze. The mitral valve snaps shut and the aortic valve is still shut, so no blood gets in or out. Pressure climbs straight up while volume stays the same: the right edge.

How the loop works · phase 3 of 4

Ejection: the top edge

Phase 3, ejection (pumping out). Once the pressure inside is higher than in the aorta, the main pipe out to the body, the aortic valve opens and blood rushes out. Volume falls while pressure stays high, so the dot runs left along the top edge.

How the loop works · phase 4 of 4

Relaxing with both valves shut: the left edge

Phase 4, isovolumic relaxation (a "same-volume relax"). The muscle lets go. The aortic valve shuts and the mitral valve is not yet open, so volume cannot change while pressure drops straight down: the left edge. Then the mitral valve opens and filling begins again.

How the loop works

One heartbeat, once round the loop

Put the four phases together and you get the pressure–volume loop: one heartbeat drawn as one lap. The dot runs round anticlockwise (against a clock's hands): fill along the bottom, squeeze up the right, pump out across the top, relax down the left.

How the loop works · the key measures

Stroke volume: the loop's width

Stroke volume is the amount of blood pumped out in one beat. On the loop it is the width: from the right edge (full, before the squeeze) to the left edge (what is left after pumping). A wider loop means more blood per beat.

How the loop works · the key measures

Ejection fraction: the share pumped out

Ejection fraction is the share of the blood in the full ventricle that one beat pumps out: the loop's width compared with the whole bar from empty to full. A healthy ventricle pumps out a little more than half of what it holds.

What shapes the loop · 1 of 3

Preload: how full before the squeeze

Preload is how much the ventricle is filled and stretched just before it squeezes. On the loop it decides where the right edge sits: more filling pushes the right edge further right, less filling pulls it back left.

What shapes the loop · 2 of 3

Afterload: what the ventricle pushes against

Afterload is how hard the ventricle must push to open the aortic valve and drive blood into the arteries, the vessels leading away from the heart. On the loop it shows in the top edge: more push-back means pumping happens at a higher pressure.

What shapes the loop · 3 of 3

Contractility: how strongly the muscle squeezes

Contractility is how strongly the heart muscle itself squeezes, whatever its filling. On the loop it decides how far the top left corner reaches: a stronger squeeze empties the ventricle further, moving that corner, and the left edge, to the left.

What changes the loop · 1 of 6

More preload: fluid through a drip

A drip puts fluid into a vein, a vessel bringing blood back to the heart. More blood returns and the ventricle fills further: more preload. Stretched muscle squeezes harder, so the loop grows right, taller and wider: more blood per beat.

What changes the loop · 2 of 6

More afterload: high blood pressure or a vessel-tightening drug

When small arteries tighten, as in high blood pressure or with a drug that narrows them (an arterial vasoconstrictor), the ventricle must push harder to get blood out: more afterload. The loop grows taller and narrower: more blood left behind, less pumped per beat.

What changes the loop · 3 of 6

More contractility: a positive inotrope

A positive inotrope is a drug that makes heart muscle squeeze more strongly: more contractility. From the same filling, the ventricle empties further, so the left edge moves left. The loop gets wider: more blood per beat, and a bigger share pumped out.

What changes the loop · 4 of 6

Less preload: a venodilator

A venodilator is a drug that widens the veins. More blood pools in them, so less returns and the ventricle fills less: less preload. The loop shrinks from the right: less blood per beat.

What changes the loop · 5 of 6

Slower and gentler: a beta-blocker

A beta-blocker is a drug that blocks the signals telling the heart to speed up and squeeze harder, so it beats slower and squeezes less hard. The loop gets shorter (lower pressure) but keeps about its width; with fewer beats, less blood is pumped each minute.

What changes the loop · 6 of 6

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

In heart failure the heart cannot pump enough for the body. In this kind the muscle is weak: low contractility. It empties poorly, so more blood is left behind and the ventricle enlarges. The loop moves right and drops: the ejection fraction falls.

About the model

What is simulated

The left ventricle is a chamber whose stiffness rises and falls each beat (time-varying elastance). Blood enters through the mitral valve from an atrium held at a fixed pressure, leaves through the aortic valve into an arterial tree that behaves like a leaky balloon (a two-element Windkessel), and drains from there into the body through a resistance.

Equations

T  = 60 / HR
Ts = min(0.16 + 0.3·T, 0.45·T)
τ  = t mod T
e(τ) = sin(π·τ / Ts) for 0 ≤ τ < Ts, else 0

Pes(V) = Ees·(V − V0)              ESPVR
Ped(V) = A·(exp(λ·(V − V0)) − 1)    EDPVR
Plv    = e·Pes(V) + (1 − e)·Ped(V)

Qmv = max(0, (Pla − Plv) / Rmv)     mitral
Qav = max(0, (Plv − Pao) / Rav)     aortic

dV/dt   = Qmv − Qav
C·dPao/dt = Qav − Pao / Rs

Integrated with fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta, dt = 0.5 ms. Fixed values: V0 = 10 mL, A = 0.18 mmHg, Rmv = 0.005 and Rav = 0.01 mmHg·s/mL. The run starts from V = 120 mL and Pao = 80 mmHg.

Reading the numbers

  • EDV is the volume at the start of each cycle; ESV is the smallest volume in the beat.
  • SV = EDV − ESV, EF = SV / EDV, output = SV × HR.
  • Stroke work is the area inside the loop, by the shoelace formula over every sampled (V, Plv) point.
  • Ea, effective arterial elastance, is end-systolic pressure divided by SV. On the plot it is the steepness of the dashed line from (EDV, 0) up to the end-systolic corner: the magnitude of its slope, since the line itself falls as volume rises.
  • Valve events come from the flows: a valve is open while blood moves through it. The loop counts as settled once EDV and ESV each move by less than 0.1 mL from one beat to the next.

Simplifications

  • Left atrial pressure is held constant and is the preload control. A real atrium fills, contracts and empties; here it is a fixed reservoir.
  • Valves are ideal one-way diodes with a small linear resistance: no regurgitation, no inertia of blood, no valve dynamics.
  • No pericardium, no right heart, no interaction between the ventricles, and no reflexes: changing one slider changes nothing else.
  • Because the mitral resistance is small, filling usually comes close to finishing before the next beat, so this model understates how a short diastole limits filling. It does fall short at high heart rates, and with a very compliant ventricle (low λ) at low preload even at normal rates.
  • The end-systolic corner lands on the ESPVR only if the aortic valve shuts near peak activation. That holds near baseline (within about 1 mmHg), but at extreme combinations, such as very low resistance and compliance with high preload, ejection runs on after the peak and the corner can fall tens of mmHg below the line. The model checks table flags this.
  • The default parameters were tuned towards the typical adult values in the Clinical values tab. Not every baseline value can sit inside its range (for example, at baseline end-diastolic pressure is approximately equal to atrial pressure, whose typical range differs), and the tab lists each place where the baseline differs. The parameters are not fitted to any person, and the model's own outputs are not reference values or thresholds.
  • When filling reaches equilibrium through the low-resistance mitral valve, as it does at baseline, the ventricle's end-diastolic pressure is approximately equal to the fixed atrial pressure. When filling is incomplete (a short diastole at high heart rates, or a very compliant ventricle at low preload), end-diastolic pressure stays below atrial pressure.
