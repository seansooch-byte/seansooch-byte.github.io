The Lab / Unit 9: Cells, membranes and signals / Lesson 25

Diffusion, osmosis
and IV fluids.

Particles spread out, and water follows the ones that cannot cross a membrane. See why that decides whether a cell swells, shrinks or keeps its size, then follow five kinds of fluid dripped into a vein to where they end up.

Educational, not for clinical use

Particles and membranes

A cell is a tiny bag of water

Your body is built from cells. Each one holds watery fluid inside the cell, wrapped in a thin, oily skin, the cell membrane. More water, the fluid outside the cell, bathes every cell from the outside.

Particles and membranes

Things dissolve in that water

Salts, sugars and proteins are dissolved in the water on both sides. Anything dissolved is a solute. Outside cells the main solute particle is sodium; inside cells it is potassium. Water fills every gap between the particles.

Particles and membranes

Diffusion: a crowd spreads out

Dissolved particles jiggle all the time, so a crowded patch spreads until it is evenly mixed, like sugar through tea. A difference in crowding from one place to another is a concentration gradient. Drifting down it, from crowded to sparse, with no energy spent, is diffusion. It stops when the crowding is level.

Particles and membranes

A membrane chooses what crosses

The cell membrane is semipermeable: some things pass, others are held back. Water crosses easily, much of it through water channels. Sodium cannot cross freely: the little that leaks in is pumped straight back out, so it behaves as if held outside.

Particles and membranes

Osmosis: water follows solute

When a solute cannot cross, water moves instead, toward the side with more of it. Water crossing a membrane toward the more crowded side is osmosis. The salt stays put; the salty side's level rises until the weight of its taller column pushes back as hard as osmosis pulls.

Particles and membranes

Only particles that stay put move water

Osmolality counts every dissolved particle in each kilogram of water. But urea, a waste product, crosses cell membranes freely, evens out and pulls no water. Particles held on one side, such as sodium, are effective osmoles: they move water. Counting only them gives tonicity, which decides whether a cell swells or shrinks.

Particles and membranes

Same tonicity on both sides: isotonic

A fluid whose tonicity matches the inside of the cell is isotonic. Water still crosses all the time, but as much moves in as moves out, so the cell keeps its size. The dashed outline is its normal size.

Particles and membranes

Fewer particles outside: hypotonic

A hypotonic fluid has fewer effective particles than the inside of the cell. Water moves into the cell by osmosis, and the cell swells. Swell far enough and a cell can burst; brain cells, boxed in by the skull, are the ones that matter most.

Particles and membranes

More particles outside: hypertonic

A hypertonic fluid has more effective particles than the inside of the cell. Water moves out of the cell by osmosis, and the cell shrinks. Water always moves toward the side with the higher tonicity.

Body water

The body's three water compartments

Body water sits in three compartments. Most is intracellular fluid, inside cells. The rest is outside cells: interstitial fluid in the spaces between cells, and plasma, the liquid part of blood, inside the vessels. The bar shows their sizes; sodium fills the fluid outside cells, potassium the fluid inside.

Body water

The capillary wall holds big molecules

The capillary wall, the thin wall of the smallest blood vessels, lets water and sodium through its gaps freely. Large molecules such as blood proteins, called colloids, mostly cannot pass while the wall is healthy. Held inside, they pull water back into the vessel: colloid osmotic pressure.

Body water

Where a drip of fluid ends up

An intravenous (IV) fluid is a liquid dripped straight into a vein. Where it ends up depends on what is dissolved in it: sodium spreads through all the fluid outside cells, protein stays mostly in the vessels, and plain water with nothing holding it, free water, goes everywhere, cells included.

What changes it

Isotonic crystalloid

A crystalloid is water with small dissolved salts. An isotonic one, such as normal saline, or lactated Ringer's (a little below it), is close to the body's own tonicity, so almost no water is pulled into or out of cells. Its sodium spreads through all the fluid outside cells and takes the water with it: the space between cells gains most, the plasma only a part.

What changes it

Hypotonic fluid

A hypotonic fluid, such as half-strength saline, carries less sodium than the body's own fluid. It dilutes the fluid outside cells, so some of its water moves into cells by osmosis and they swell; brain cells, boxed in by the skull, are most at risk. Only its share of plain water does that. The rest stays outside cells like an isotonic fluid, so the vessels keep less than they would of an isotonic fluid.

What changes it

Hypertonic fluid

Hypertonic saline carries more sodium than the body's own fluid. It concentrates the fluid outside cells, so water moves out of cells by osmosis. Cells shrink, while the fluid outside them, plasma included, gains more water than was dripped in. That pull is why it is used to draw water out of a swollen brain.

What changes it

Colloid

A colloid fluid, such as albumin, carries large molecules that mostly stay behind a healthy capillary wall. Their pull holds much of its water inside the vessels, so the plasma fills more than with the same amount of crystalloid, and cells barely change. A leaky wall, as in burns or severe infection, lets the molecules out.

What changes it

Free water: a glucose solution once the sugar is used

D5W is glucose, a sugar, in water. In the bag it is about as concentrated as blood, but cells take the sugar up and use it, leaving free water. That spreads into every compartment, most of it into cells, which swell a little. In a real body the dilution switches ADH off, so the kidney passes the extra water (Lesson 22).

    How this teaching model works

    Body water is one whole split into three compartments: inside cells, between cells and plasma. The split comes from the Clinical values data file. Every compartment starts at the same concentration of particles that cannot cross a cell membrane.

    A fluid brings salt at some strength compared with the body's own fluid, sugar, and, for a colloid, large molecules. Salt stays outside cells. Sugar counts as a particle until the share of it that cells take up is used. The salt slider sets a plain salt fluid; the colloid and the glucose solution come only as presets in the fluid list.

    Water moves until every compartment shares one concentration: the particles inside cells plus those outside, divided by all the water. The water inside cells is then the particles inside divided by that concentration; the rest is outside.

    Outside cells, water splits between plasma and the space between cells in the normal proportion, except that a colloid's own water stays in plasma. That assumes a healthy capillary wall holding all of it back; real large molecules are mostly, not completely, held back, and a leaky wall lets them escape. Protein pull is the plasma protein divided by plasma water. Sodium is the sodium outside cells divided by the water outside cells.

    Tonicity is its baseline times the shared concentration. Total osmolality is tonicity plus the freely crossing particles, such as urea, spread through all body water, so it never falls below tonicity. The model leaves out the kidney, thirst, hormones such as ADH, lymph flow and time, all of which correct these shifts in a real body.
