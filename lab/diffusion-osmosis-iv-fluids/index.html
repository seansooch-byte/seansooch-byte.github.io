Diffusion, osmosis and IV fluids.

How water and a dissolved substance cross a membrane, and why the make-up of a fluid dripped into a vein decides whether a cell swells, shrinks or holds its size.

Educational, not for clinical use. No treatment amounts appear anywhere in this lesson.

Builds toward: Lesson 22, ADH and Water Balance. Related: Lesson 2, The Nephron and Diuretics · Lesson 1, The Pressure–Volume Loop (fluids add filling).

Step 1 of 16 Building the idea

    A cell is a tiny bag of water

    Your body is built from cells. Each one is a tiny bag of watery fluid wrapped in a thin skin, the cell membrane: the boundary between inside and outside. More fluid bathes every cell from the outside.

    Things dissolve in that water

    Salts, sugars and proteins are dissolved in the water inside and outside the cell. Anything dissolved is a solute. Each orange dot here is a solute particle; water fills every gap around them.

    Diffusion: a crowd spreads out

    Particles jiggle all the time, so a crowded patch spreads until it is evenly mixed, like sugar spreading through tea. This drift from crowded to sparse, with no energy spent, is diffusion. Press Replay to watch again.

    A membrane chooses what crosses

    The cell membrane is semipermeable: some things pass, others are held back. Water crosses easily. Most solutes, such as salt particles, cannot cross on their own, so they stay on the side where they started.

    Osmosis: water follows solute

    When solutes cannot cross, water moves instead, toward the side with more solute. This diffusion of water across a membrane is osmosis. The salty side’s level rises until the weight of its taller column pushes back as hard as osmosis pulls; the salt stays put.

    Same on both sides: isotonic

    Tonicity compares the solutes that cannot cross a cell membrane outside the cell with those inside it. A fluid that matches the cell is isotonic: water moves in and out equally, so the cell holds its size.

    Fewer solutes outside: hypotonic

    A hypotonic fluid has fewer solute particles than the inside of the cell. Water moves into the cell by osmosis, and the cell swells. Swell too far and a cell can burst. The dashed outline is its normal size.

    More solutes outside: hypertonic

    A hypertonic fluid has more solute particles than the inside of the cell. Water moves out of the cell by osmosis, and the cell shrinks and wrinkles. The dashed outline is its normal size.

    The body’s three water rooms

    Body water sits in three compartments. Most is inside cells. The rest is outside cells: in the gaps between cells, and in plasma, the liquid part of blood, inside the vessels. The bar shows their relative sizes.

    The vessel wall holds big molecules

    The wall of the smallest blood vessels lets water and salt pass freely. Very large molecules, such as blood proteins, mostly cannot pass while the wall is intact; these are colloids. Held in the vessel, they keep pulling water back in.

    Where a drip of fluid ends up

    An intravenous (IV) fluid is a liquid dripped straight into a vein. Where it ends up depends on what is dissolved in it. Two things to watch from here on: the size of the cells, and how full the vessels are.

    • Salt stays outside cells; an isotonic salt fluid keeps its water there too
    • Colloid mostly stays in the vessel
    • Plain water goes everywhere, cells included

    Isotonic crystalloid

    A crystalloid is water with small dissolved salts. An isotonic one matches the body’s own fluid, so no water is pulled into or out of cells. It spreads through all the space outside cells, filling the vessels only partly.

    Hypotonic fluid

    A hypotonic fluid carries less salt than the body’s own fluid. It dilutes the fluid outside cells, so water moves into cells by osmosis and they swell. The vessels keep only a little of it.

    Dashed: normalSolid: after a hypotonic fluid

    Hypertonic fluid

    A hypertonic fluid carries more salt than the body’s own fluid. It concentrates the fluid outside cells, so water moves out of cells by osmosis. Cells shrink while the space outside them, vessels included, grows.

    Dashed: normalSolid: after a hypertonic fluid

    Colloid

    A colloid fluid carries large molecules that mostly stay behind an intact vessel wall. They hold much of its water inside the vessels, so the vessels fill more than with a crystalloid, while the space between cells and the cells barely change.

    Free water and glucose solutions

    Free water is water with nothing dissolved. Shown here: glucose, a sugar, in water, made as concentrated as blood. Cells take up and use the sugar, leaving free water, which spreads into every compartment, mostly into cells. They swell a little.

      How the model works

      Body water is one unit split into three rooms: inside cells (I), between cells (B) and plasma (P). The split comes from the Clinical values file. Every room starts with one unit of concentration.

      A fluid of volume V brings salt at strength s (relative to the body’s fluid), sugar at strength g and, for a colloid, large molecules. Salt stays outside cells. Sugar counts as a particle until a share u is taken up by cells and used.

      Water moves until all rooms share one concentration: c = (particles inside + particles outside) ÷ (1 + V), where particles outside = (B + P) + V·s + V·g·(1 − u). Then inside = I ÷ c and outside = 1 + V − inside.

      Outside cells, water splits between plasma and the space between cells in the normal proportion, except that a colloid’s own volume stays in plasma. That assumes an intact vessel wall holding all of it back; real large molecules are mostly, not completely, held back, and a leaky wall (as in burns) lets them escape. Protein pull is protein ÷ plasma volume; a colloid brings its own pull at plasma strength. Sodium is (B + P + V·s) ÷ outside volume.

      Clinical markers start from the model baseline in the data file, chosen inside each cited range. Tonicity is its baseline × c. Total osmolality is tonicity plus the freely crossing particles, such as urea, spread through all body water: baseline difference ÷ (1 + V), so it can never fall below tonicity. The model leaves out the kidney, thirst, hormones, lymph flow and time, all of which correct these shifts in a real body. The pictures draw changes larger than life so they can be seen.
