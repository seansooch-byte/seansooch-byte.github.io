The Lab / Unit 6: Inflammation, infection, immunity and cancer / Lesson 19

The bacterial cell and its antibiotic targets.

A bacterium is a single cell with a few parts your own cells lack or build differently: a woven wall, its own protein factories, a coiled loop of instructions and a home-made vitamin. Take one apart, piece by piece, then watch six families of antibiotics each break a different piece.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

A bacterium is one tiny living cell

A bacterium (plural bacteria) is a living thing made of a single cell, far smaller than one of your own cells. Most bacteria are harmless, but some cause infections: they get into the body and multiply where they should not.

Build the idea

The membrane holds everything in

Like your cells, a bacterium is wrapped in a thin, oily skin, its cell membrane. It keeps the watery inside in, and lets food in and waste out through small gates.

Build the idea

The wall: a net around the balloon

Outside the membrane, most bacteria have a tough cell wall of peptidoglycan: sugar chains tied by short bridges, like a woven net. Water keeps seeping in, so the inside presses out; the wall stops the cell swelling until it bursts. Your cells have no wall at all.

Build the idea

Building the wall, bridge by bridge

Proteins, chains of small building blocks, do the cell’s work; most enzymes are proteins that each speed up one chemical reaction. New wall bricks arrive with a two-part tip, D-Ala-D-Ala. Wall-building enzymes, also called penicillin-binding proteins, grip the tip, cut off the last D-Ala and tie the brick in with a fresh bridge.

Build the idea

Two kinds of wall

A purple stain sorts bacteria in two. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick wall on the outside and stay purple. Gram-negative bacteria stain pink: their thin wall is hidden under an extra outer membrane, a second oily skin that keeps many large drug molecules out.

Build the idea

DNA: the instruction loop

Inside floats the bacterium’s DNA, the long molecule that carries its instructions for building every part. Your DNA sits in a walled-off centre, the nucleus; in most bacteria it is one loop, coiled up tightly and loose in the cell.

Build the idea

Untangling enzymes let DNA be copied

Copying the loop winds the DNA ahead tighter and tighter. DNA gyrase eases it: it cuts both strands, passes another stretch of DNA through the gap and reseals it. Topoisomerase IV does the same to unhook the two finished copies so they can separate. These are the bacterium’s untangling enzymes.

Build the idea

Ribosomes build proteins

Ribosomes are the cell’s protein factories. Building blocks dock at the small part, the 30S subunit, which reads a copied instruction. The large part, the 50S subunit, links them into a protein chain that leaves through an exit. Bacterial ribosomes are built differently from yours, so a drug can grip theirs and largely spare yours.

Build the idea

Folate: a vitamin it makes itself

Copying DNA needs fresh DNA letters, and making some of them, the purines and thymidine, needs folate, a vitamin. You get folate from food. Many bacteria cannot take it in, so they build their own, one enzyme per step. Two enzymes matter here: an earlier one, dihydropteroate synthase, and a later one, dihydrofolate reductase.

Build the idea

Grow, split, repeat

With its wall, proteins and copied DNA ready, the bacterium grows and pinches in two. Each half grows and splits again, so their numbers double and double. This growth is what lets an infection spread, and it needs every part you have met.

Build the idea

The whole bacterium: four targets

An antibiotic is a medicine that harms bacteria by hitting a part your cells lack or build differently. Four parts make good targets: the wall, the ribosomes, the DNA untangling enzymes and the folate line. Each family of antibiotics strikes one of them.

Build the idea

Kill or stop: the key measures

Some antibiotics kill the bacteria they reach: they are bactericidal. Others mainly stop them multiplying: they are bacteriostatic, and your white blood cells, the body’s defence cells (Lesson 18), clear them. Which one a drug is depends on the drug, the germ and the conditions. Key measures: growth, and bacteria alive.

What changes it

Beta-lactams: the bridges stop

Beta-lactams, such as penicillins and cephalosporins, are shaped like the D-Ala-D-Ala tip, so they lodge in the wall-building enzymes and jam them. New bridges stop forming while the bacterium keeps growing and reshaping its wall, so the wall weakens, the inside pushes out and the cell bursts. They kill bacteria that are growing.

What changes it

Vancomycin: the brick tips covered

Glycopeptides such as vancomycin leave the enzymes alone and clamp onto the D-Ala-D-Ala tip of each new brick instead, so the enzymes cannot tie it in. Wall building fails, which kills many growing gram-positive bacteria, though not all: enterococci are usually only stopped. The molecule is too big to pass an outer membrane, so gram-negative bacteria escape.

What changes it

Aminoglycosides: the instructions misread

Aminoglycosides, such as gentamicin, grip the small 30S part of the ribosome so it misreads the instructions. Faulty proteins pile up and punch holes in the membrane, and the bacterium dies. They are used mostly against gram-negative bacteria.

What changes it

Macrolides and tetracyclines: the factory stalls

Macrolides, such as azithromycin, plug the exit of the large 50S part, where the chain leaves. Tetracyclines, such as doxycycline, block the docking site on the small 30S part, so building blocks cannot dock. Protein making stalls and growth stops, but most bacteria stay alive for white blood cells to clear.

What changes it

Fluoroquinolones: the DNA left cut

Fluoroquinolones, such as ciprofloxacin, trap DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV just after they cut the DNA. The cuts are never sealed, so copying halts and the broken DNA kills the bacterium.

What changes it

Sulfonamides and trimethoprim: folate cut off

Sulfonamides mimic the raw material and block the earlier step; trimethoprim blocks the later one. With little folate, too few purines and thymidine are made, so copying and growth stall, and two blocks on one line work better than either alone. Your cells take folate from food, and trimethoprim grips the bacterial dihydrofolate reductase far more tightly than yours.

    How this teaching model works

    Each drug blocks one piece of the machinery by a fixed illustrative share. Beta-lactams and vancomycin stop new wall bridges; aminoglycosides make the ribosome misread, so proteins come out faulty; macrolides and tetracyclines stall the ribosome; fluoroquinolones leave the DNA cut; sulfonamides with trimethoprim cut off folate, so fewer DNA letters are made. Growth needs working proteins and copied DNA. A weak wall does not stop growth by itself; it makes growing bacteria burst.

    Killing comes from three routes: a weak wall in a growing cell, faulty proteins, and cut DNA. Because wall drugs need growth to kill, a growth-stopping drug given with them blunts their killing in this model, a well-known teaching point. A drug that only stops growth leaves the bacteria alive; white blood cells then clear them slowly. Vancomycin does not reach the wall of a gram-negative bacterium at all in this model, because it is too big to pass the intact outer membrane, so on its own it leaves that bacterium and its growth curve unchanged; aminoglycosides reach gram-positive bacteria less well; the beta-lactam-cutting enzyme stands for a common kind of resistance.

    Over time, the bacteria grow toward a ceiling, a few are cleared by white blood cells, and drugs start partway along. Left out: how drugs are absorbed, spread and cleared (Lesson 17), every other kind of resistance, the many differences between bacterial species, side effects, and every real-world difference between people. Nothing here is a prediction for a real infection, and nothing here is a treatment plan.
