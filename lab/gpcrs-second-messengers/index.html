The Lab / Unit 9: Cells, membranes and signals / Lesson 31

GPCRs and
second messengers.

A hormone that never enters a cell can still change what happens inside it. Follow the message from a receptor in the membrane, through a G protein switch, to a crowd of messengers inside, then see cholera toxin jam the switch on and pertussis toxin jam it off.

Educational, not for clinical use

A signal at the surface

A message that cannot get in

Cells talk with chemical signals. A hormone is a signal carried in the blood, such as adrenaline. Many hormones cannot cross the cell membrane, the thin, oily skin around every cell. They stay outside, so the hormone is called the first messenger: something else must carry its message inside.

A signal at the surface

A receptor threads through the membrane

A receptor is a protein that one signal fits, like a key in a lock. This kind crosses the membrane seven times, with a binding pocket open to the outside and loops hanging inside the cell. It is called a G protein-coupled receptor, or GPCR, after the partner it works through, met two steps on.

A signal at the surface

The hormone fits, and the receptor changes shape

The molecule that settles into a receptor's pocket is its ligand; here, the hormone. Binding shifts the receptor's seven coils, and the inside end of one of them swings outward. Nothing has entered the cell: only the receptor's shape has changed, and that new shape is the message.

A signal at the surface

A G protein waits underneath

Just under the receptor, tethered to the inside of the membrane, sits a G protein, built of three parts called alpha, beta and gamma. Alpha can hold a small molecule called GDP, and a G protein whose alpha holds GDP is switched off. With no hormone bound, almost every G protein sits like this; only an occasional one switches on by chance.

A signal at the surface

Swapping GDP for GTP switches it on

The reshaped receptor makes alpha let go of its GDP and pick up GTP, a close cousin that carries one more phosphate group (a small cluster of phosphorus and oxygen atoms). Holding GTP, alpha is switched on: it leaves beta and gamma and slides along the inside of the membrane to its target. One bound receptor can switch on several G proteins in turn.

A signal at the surface

The G protein switches itself off

Alpha carries its own timer. It slowly splits its GTP back to GDP, cutting off the extra phosphate. Holding GDP again, it is switched off and rejoins beta and gamma under the receptor. So once the hormone has gone, the signal stops by itself, with nothing else needed.

Second messengers

Adenylyl cyclase makes cyclic AMP

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one chemical reaction. Switched-on alpha of a stimulating G protein works an enzyme in the membrane, adenylyl cyclase. It turns ATP, the cell's energy molecule, into cyclic AMP. Made inside the cell, cyclic AMP carries the hormone's message on: it is a second messenger.

Second messengers

One signal, many messengers

Each step multiplies the one before. One bound receptor switches on several G proteins. Each switched-on alpha keeps its cyclase working, and each cyclase makes cyclic AMP over and over. So one hormone molecule outside becomes a crowd of messengers inside. That is why a tiny amount of hormone in the blood is enough.

Second messengers

Cyclic AMP switches on protein kinase A

A kinase is an enzyme that sticks a phosphate group onto other proteins, which switches them on or off. Cyclic AMP's main target is protein kinase A, held idle by two braking parts. Cyclic AMP binds the brakes, the two working parts are freed, and they add phosphates to the cell's own proteins: a channel, a pump, an enzyme.

Second messengers

Phosphodiesterase clears cyclic AMP

The messenger is cleared all the time. An enzyme called phosphodiesterase opens cyclic AMP's ring, leaving plain AMP, which carries no message. So the level of cyclic AMP is a balance: how fast the cyclase makes it against how fast phosphodiesterase clears it. Make more, or clear less, and the level rises.

Second messengers

Three kinds of G protein

G proteins come in families, named for what their alpha does. Gs (s for stimulating) switches adenylyl cyclase on, so cyclic AMP rises. Gi (i for inhibiting) brakes it, so cyclic AMP falls. Gq works phospholipase C, an enzyme that cuts a fat of the membrane, and ends in a rise of calcium. This lesson shows each receptor with its main family; some receptors can work with more than one.

Second messengers

Phospholipase C splits a membrane lipid

Switched-on Gq alpha works phospholipase C, which cuts one particular fat of the membrane in two. One half, DAG, stays in the membrane. The other half, IP3, is small, dissolves in the cell's fluid and drifts away: a second messenger of its own.

Second messengers

IP3 opens the calcium store

A cell keeps calcium very low in its fluid and stores it in the endoplasmic reticulum, a network of membrane sacs. IP3 binds channels in that membrane and opens them, so calcium floods out into the cell's fluid. Calcium then does the work: it makes muscle cells contract and gland cells release what they hold.

What changes it

Gs: more cyclic AMP

Adrenaline at a beta-1 receptor on a heart pacemaker cell switches on Gs. Alpha drives adenylyl cyclase, and cyclic AMP rises to more than four times its resting level. Through protein kinase A and the pacemaker's channels, the cell fires sooner after each beat, so the heart speeds up (Lesson 6). The same Gs path raises cyclic AMP in many other cells.

What changes it

Gi: less cyclic AMP

Acetylcholine from the vagus nerve, at an M2 receptor on the same pacemaker cell, switches on Gi. Its alpha brakes adenylyl cyclase, and cyclic AMP falls to less than half its resting level; Gi's beta and gamma also open potassium channels. The pacemaker fires later after each beat, so the heart slows (Lesson 7).

What changes it

Gq: IP3 and a rise in cell calcium

Noradrenaline at an alpha-1 receptor on the smooth muscle of a blood vessel switches on Gq. Phospholipase C makes IP3, IP3 opens the calcium store, and calcium in the cell's fluid rises to several times its resting level. The muscle cell contracts, so the vessel narrows. Cyclic AMP does not change: this family works a different enzyme.

What changes it

Cholera toxin: Gs locked on

Cholera toxin, made by the bacterium that causes cholera, enters gut lining cells and tags Gs alpha so it can no longer split its GTP. Gs stays on with no hormone at all, and cyclic AMP climbs to almost six times normal and stays there. Protein kinase A opens the CFTR chloride channel (Lesson 30); chloride, sodium and water pour into the gut: watery diarrhea.

What changes it

Pertussis toxin: Gi locked off

Pertussis toxin, from the bacterium that causes whooping cough, tags Gi alpha so a receptor can no longer make it swap GDP for GTP. Acetylcholine still binds its M2 receptor, but Gi stays off. In a healthy cell the same signal cuts cyclic AMP to less than half; here it stays at about its resting level. The brake is gone, rather than the accelerator stuck.

    How this teaching model works

    Each G protein's alpha is either off, holding GDP, or on, holding GTP. It is switched on at a small resting rate plus a rate that grows with the hormone at its receptor, and it switches itself off at a steady rate by splitting GTP. The share of alpha that is on settles where switching on balances switching off.

    Each toxin slider sets the share of its target that is tagged, and the model treats the tagged and untagged G proteins as two separate groups. Tagged Gs cannot split its GTP, so once it picks up GTP it stays on, hormone or not: the model counts all of it as on. Tagged Gi cannot swap GDP for GTP, so it stays off whatever reaches its receptor. The untagged rest switch on and off as normal, and the share of each family that is on is the two groups added together.

    Adenylyl cyclase runs at a resting rate, faster with Gs on and slower with Gi on. Phosphodiesterase clears cyclic AMP at a steady rate, so cyclic AMP is proportional to the cyclase's rate. Phospholipase C runs faster with Gq on; IP3 follows it, and calcium in the cell's fluid rises twice as steeply as IP3. Every level is shown relative to the resting cell.

    Read as a gut lining cell, cyclic AMP sets how far the CFTR chloride channel is opened, so the model's chloride secretion follows it. Read as a heart pacemaker cell, the rate rises with the fourth root of cyclic AMP from the receptor signals alone. The model leaves out receptors wearing out with long use, the cell making more phosphodiesterase, and everything outside the cell.
