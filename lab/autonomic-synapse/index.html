The Lab / Unit 2: Nerves, rhythm and muscle / Lesson 6

The autonomic
synapse.

Your organs are wired to an accelerator and a brake. Watch one signal leave a nerve, cross a gap and dock in a receptor, learn which receptor does what in each organ, then see how seven kinds of drugs press, release or jam those pedals.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the signal

Nerves that run you on autopilot

Some nerves work without you ever thinking about them. They make up the autonomic nervous system: wiring from the brain and spinal cord to the organs that keep you alive, such as the heart, blood vessels, lungs, eyes and gut. Signals run along it all the time.

Build the signal

Two branches, pulling opposite ways

The sympathetic branch (red, solid) is the accelerator: it readies you for “fight or flight”. The parasympathetic branch (green, dashed) is the brake: it runs “rest and digest”. Most organs get both, and both work all the time, a little more or less. Most blood vessels get only the accelerator.

Build the signal

Nerve and organ never quite touch

Near an organ, an autonomic nerve fibre has many small swellings, its nerve endings, that stop just short of the organ’s cells. Each swelling and the tiny gap beside it form a synapse. When a signal arrives, the swelling releases a neurotransmitter: a chemical messenger that crosses the gap in a split second, docks on the organ cell and passes the signal on. Then the gap is cleared.

Build the signal

The brake’s messenger: acetylcholine

Parasympathetic nerves release acetylcholine (green dots). It works only where it can dock on a receptor: a site on the cell shaped to fit one messenger, like a lock for a key. On organs, its receptors are muscarinic receptors, M for short.

Build the signal

An enzyme keeps the signal short

An enzyme is a protein that makes one chemical change happen. Cholinesterase, waiting in the gap, chops acetylcholine apart within moments. So the brake acts only while the nerve keeps firing, and lets go soon after it stops.

Build the signal

The accelerator’s messenger: noradrenaline

Most sympathetic nerves release noradrenaline (red dots), also called norepinephrine; those to sweat glands release acetylcholine instead. Noradrenaline docks on adrenergic receptors, which come in two families, alpha and beta, each with numbered types such as alpha-1 and beta-1. The types an organ carries decide how it answers.

Build the signal

Noradrenaline is pumped back in

No enzyme in this gap chops noradrenaline up. Instead the nerve ending pumps most of it back inside to use again: reuptake. Some docks on alpha-2 receptors on the ending itself, which turn further release down, a brake built into the accelerator. Lesson 4’s central alpha-2 agonists switch on this receptor type in the brainstem.

The organs answer

Acetylcholine slows the heart

The heart has its own pacemaker, which left alone would beat faster than it does at rest. Acetylcholine on its M receptors holds the heart rate, the beats each minute, down, like a foot resting on the brake. Each dot on the strip is one beat, so closer dots mean a faster rate.

The organs answer

Beta-1 receptors speed and strengthen the heart

Through beta-1 receptors on the heart, noradrenaline does the opposite of acetylcholine: the heart beats faster, and each beat squeezes harder, drawn as bigger dots. More blood is pumped every minute.

The organs answer

Alpha-1 receptors squeeze the smallest arteries

Arterioles are the smallest arteries, ringed with muscle. Through alpha-1 receptors on that muscle, noradrenaline tightens it, so they narrow. Blood is harder to push through, and blood pressure, the push of blood on artery walls, rises.

The organs answer

The adrenal glands add adrenaline to the blood

Sympathetic nerves also reach the adrenal glands, small glands on top of the kidneys. They answer by releasing adrenaline (epinephrine), a close cousin of noradrenaline, into the blood. It reaches receptors far from any sympathetic nerve, such as the beta-2 receptors on airway muscle.

The organs answer

The pupil: a tug of war

The pupil is the dark opening of the eye. A ring muscle with M receptors makes it smaller when acetylcholine arrives; a spoke-like muscle with alpha-1 receptors makes it larger when noradrenaline arrives. Its size shows which branch is winning.

The organs answer

The airways: acetylcholine narrows, adrenaline widens

The airways carry air into the lungs, wrapped in muscle. Acetylcholine from parasympathetic nerves, on M receptors, tightens it and narrows them. Sympathetic nerves barely reach this muscle; adrenaline in the blood relaxes it through beta-2 receptors, widening them. Asthma inhalers either switch on beta-2 receptors or block M receptors.

The organs answer

Pressure sensors close the loop

Sensors in the artery walls feel blood pressure and report to the brainstem. If pressure rises, it eases the accelerator and presses the brake, so the heart slows and pressure falls back; if pressure falls, the reverse. This automatic answer is the baroreflex. Lesson 4 covers it.

The organs answer

The whole picture

Two nerve messengers, one hormone and four receptor types on organs. Acetylcholine on M receptors slows the heart and narrows the airways and pupil. Noradrenaline on beta-1 speeds and strengthens the heart, and on alpha-1 narrows arterioles and widens the pupil. Adrenaline on beta-2 widens the airways. Key measures: heart rate and blood pressure.

What changes it

Muscarinic agonists: the brake pressed harder

An agonist is a drug that docks on a receptor and switches it on, mimicking the messenger. Muscarinic agonists, such as pilocarpine, act like extra acetylcholine on every M receptor: the heart slows and blood pressure falls, the pupil and airways narrow, and the gut and glands get busier.

What changes it

Muscarinic antagonists: the brake taken off

An antagonist, or blocker, docks on a receptor without switching it on, so the messenger cannot get in. Muscarinic antagonists, such as atropine, release the brake: the heart speeds up, the pupil and airways widen, and the mouth and gut go dry and quiet.

What changes it

Cholinesterase inhibitors: acetylcholine lingers

An inhibitor is a drug that stops an enzyme working. Cholinesterase inhibitors leave acetylcholine unchopped, so it builds up and keeps docking wherever it is released. M effects grow: the heart slows, and the pupils and airways narrow. At skeletal muscle a moderate build-up strengthens the signal, but too much causes weakness (Lesson 8).

What changes it

Alpha-1 agonists: arterioles squeezed

Alpha-1 agonists, such as phenylephrine, switch on alpha-1 receptors. Arterioles narrow and blood pressure rises; the baroreflex then slows the heart. The pupil widens, which is why eye drops of this kind help a doctor see inside the eye.

What changes it

Alpha-1 blockers: arterioles let go

Alpha-1 blockers keep noradrenaline off alpha-1 receptors. Arteriole muscle relaxes, the arterioles widen and blood pressure falls. The baroreflex presses the accelerator, so the heart speeds up through beta-1 receptors, which are still free. The pupil narrows. Standing up quickly can make you dizzy.

What changes it

Nonselective beta agonists: a faster heart, wider airways

Nonselective beta agonists, such as isoproterenol, switch on beta-1 and beta-2 receptors alike. Through beta-1 the heart beats faster and harder; through beta-2 the airways widen and some arterioles relax, so blood pressure need not rise and can even fall. Asthma inhalers use beta-2 agonists, which spare the heart more.

What changes it

Beta-blockers: the accelerator held back

Beta-blockers keep noradrenaline off beta receptors. Through beta-1, the heart beats slower and less forcefully, and blood pressure falls. Nonselective ones also block beta-2, so adrenaline can no longer relax airway muscle and the airways can narrow, a danger in asthma. Beta-1-selective ones spare the airways more.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is relative to a normal resting state, where every quantity equals 1. Each branch has a drive (how hard its nerves fire). Acetylcholine in the gap is the parasympathetic drive divided by how fast cholinesterase clears it. The muscarinic signal is acetylcholine plus any muscarinic agonist, times the share of muscarinic receptors left free; the alpha and beta signals are built the same way from noradrenaline. Heart rate rises with the beta signal and falls with the muscarinic signal; the heart’s squeeze strength rises with the beta signal; arteriole tightness rises with the alpha signal and eases a little with beta signals from adrenaline and any beta agonist, standing in for the beta-2 receptors that relax some blood vessels. Blood pressure is the product of squeeze strength, arteriole tightness and the square root of heart rate. In the picture each heartbeat is a dot: closer dots are a faster rate and bigger dots a stronger squeeze; it is an abstract beat counter, not a heart tracing. With the reflex on, pressure above normal lowers the sympathetic drive and raises the parasympathetic drive to the heart and arterioles (and the reverse), and the model finds the steady state where the pressure the reflex feels equals the pressure produced. Pupil and airway width are the ratio of their widening signal to their narrowing muscarinic signal, at resting drive; the reflex is not applied to them. The pupil widens through alpha-1 receptors fed by sympathetic nerves. Airway muscle has almost no sympathetic nerves, so its beta-2 signal comes from adrenaline released into the blood by the adrenal glands, which the model ties to the sympathetic drive slider. The model treats each drug as acting on every receptor of its family; real drugs often favour one type (for example beta-1 in the heart, beta-2 in the airways). It leaves out the gut, glands, sweat glands, alpha-2 receptors and reuptake, adrenaline’s own action on the heart, nicotinic receptors and anything about timing.
