The Lab / Unit 9: Cells, membranes and signals / Lesson 26

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump
and digoxin.

A protein in every cell membrane spends energy to keep sodium outside and potassium inside. See how a heart cell uses that sodium gradient to clear calcium, then watch digoxin jam the pump so calcium builds up and the heart squeezes harder.

Educational, not for clinical use

Two ions, two sides

A heart muscle cell and its membrane

Every heartbeat is made by heart muscle cells squeezing. Each cell is wrapped in a thin, oily skin, the cell membrane. It separates the fluid outside the cell from the watery inside of the cell. Charged particles called ions cannot pass through its oily middle on their own.

Two ions, two sides

Sodium outside, potassium inside

Two ions matter most here. Sodium (Na⁺) is crowded outside the cell and scarce inside. Potassium (K⁺) is the reverse: crowded inside, scarce outside. In the pictures sodium is an amber circle and potassium a green triangle.

Two ions, two sides

Ions leak down their gradients

A difference in crowding across the membrane is a concentration gradient. Ions drift down it, from crowded to sparse, through channels: protein tunnels through the membrane. So sodium slowly leaks in and potassium slowly leaks out. Left alone, the leaks would even the two sides out.

The pump

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump spends ATP

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is a protein in the membrane that undoes the leaks. It works against the gradients, so it needs energy: each cycle it splits one ATP, the cell's fuel molecule. Every cell has a great many of these pumps; the picture shows one.

The pump

Three sodium out, two potassium in

Each cycle the pump takes three sodium ions from inside, splits ATP, turns to face outside and lets them go. Then it picks up two potassium ions from outside and turns back to release them inside. Three sodium out, two potassium in, many times every second.

The pump

Pump and leaks hold the levels steady

Leaks and pump run at the same time. When the pump carries out as much sodium as leaks in, and brings in as much potassium as leaks out, the levels hold still: a steady state. Slow the pump, and sodium builds up inside until the leak and the slower pump match again.

From sodium to calcium

The sodium-calcium exchanger

Heart cells use the sodium gradient to clear calcium. The sodium-calcium exchanger lets three sodium ions flow in, down their gradient, and uses that push to throw one calcium ion out. It spends no ATP of its own: the steeper the sodium gradient, the harder it pushes calcium out.

From sodium to calcium

Calcium makes the cell squeeze

Each beat, a little calcium enters and sets off a flood from a store inside the cell, the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium lets the contractile filaments slide over each other, and the cell shortens. Then calcium is pumped back into the store, and the exchanger sends the extra out. A fuller store means a harder squeeze.

From sodium to calcium

An electrical signal starts each squeeze

Between beats the inside of the cell sits negative: its resting potential. When the voltage is pushed up to a trigger level, the threshold, the cell fires an action potential: a quick rise, a long plateau and a fall back to rest. Its plateau lets in the calcium that starts the squeeze (Lesson 7).

From sodium to calcium

The sodium gradient keeps calcium in check

Put it together. The pump keeps sodium low inside, so a steep sodium gradient drives sodium in through the exchanger, and that push keeps calcium low between beats. The store holds a normal load, and each beat squeezes with normal strength. Change the pump, and the calcium follows.

Digoxin

Digoxin jams the pump from outside

Digoxin is a cardiac glycoside, a medicine first taken from the foxglove plant. It fits into a pocket on the pump's outer face, the digoxin pocket, and jams the pump there. Potassium binds the pump at its own sites nearby, and potassium on the pump makes digoxin bind less readily: the more potassium around, the less digoxin sticks.

What changes it

Cardiac glycosides: digoxin slows the pump

In this model, digoxin jams about a third of the pumps. Sodium leaks in as before but is carried out more slowly, so it builds up inside. The sodium gradient is smaller, the exchanger pushes less calcium out, the store fills further and each beat squeezes harder. That is how digoxin helps a weak heart pump (Lesson 3).

What changes it

Hypokalemia: digoxin binds more

Hypokalemia is a low potassium level in the blood, often from diuretics or vomiting. With less potassium on the pumps to hold it off, the same digoxin binds more readily: about half the pumps are jammed instead of a third. Sodium and calcium build up further, and the risk of digoxin toxicity rises even though the digoxin has not changed.

What changes it

Hyperkalemia: digoxin binds less

Hyperkalemia is a high potassium level in the blood. More potassium on the pumps makes digoxin bind less readily: about a fifth of the pumps are jammed instead of a third, and the squeeze gains less. In severe digoxin poisoning it also works the other way: so many pumps are jammed that potassium stays outside cells, and blood potassium rises.

What changes it

Too much digoxin: delayed afterdepolarizations

Too much digoxin jams more than half the pumps and overfills the store with calcium. Between beats the store spills some out, and the exchanger clears it by bringing three sodium in for each calcium: a net inward flow of charge. The voltage bumps up after the beat, a delayed afterdepolarization. A bump that reaches threshold fires an extra beat, which can set off dangerous rhythms.

    How this teaching model works

    Digoxin binds a pocket on the pump's outer face, and potassium bound to the pump makes that binding weaker. The share of pumps jammed is the digoxin amount times a binding strength that falls with the square of the potassium outside, over one plus that product: at the baseline potassium the lesson's usual amount jams a third. Potassium outside also switches the working pumps on, half-on at a low level, so a very low potassium slows them a little by itself.

    Sodium inside builds up until the leak matches the slower pumps: it rises as the working share of pumps falls, by a power set in the lesson's data file. The calcium store follows the square of the sodium inside, because a smaller sodium gradient lets the exchanger clear less calcium; the squeeze follows the square root of the store. Every number the model uses, its illustrative parameters included, is in that one data file, marked as a teaching choice rather than a reference value.

    When the store passes a spill point, calcium spills between beats and the exchanger's inward current bumps the voltage up after each beat. The resting potential moves with the potassium outside. If the resting potential plus the bump reaches the threshold, the model fires an extra beat.

    The model leaves out the kidney, the nerves to the heart, how digoxin slows the AV node, and time: real digoxin levels build and fall over days. The digoxin amount has no unit: it is a model setting, never an amount to give anyone.
