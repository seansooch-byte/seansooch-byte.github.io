The Lab / Unit 3: Breath and blood / Lesson 12

The coagulation
cascade.

A torn blood vessel seals itself in three moves: it squeezes, plugs the hole, then knits the plug into a tough patch. Coagulation means clotting; the cascade is the chain reaction that builds the clot. Build it one picture at a time, then see where blood thinners and clot-busting drugs step in.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

Blood must flow, and leaks must seal

Blood flows through tubes called vessels. It must stay liquid to keep flowing, yet stop leaking the moment a vessel tears. Hemostasis is the body’s way of sealing that leak. It has three steps, built up here one at a time.

Build the idea

Step one: the vessel squeezes

Muscle in the vessel wall clamps down around the tear. This vascular spasm narrows the tube so less blood escapes, buying time. It is set off by chemicals from the damaged lining and by pain sensors.

Build the idea

Platelets: tiny fragments on patrol

Platelets are tiny fragments of cells, much smaller than red blood cells, carried in the blood in huge numbers. Normally they slide past the smooth vessel lining without sticking to anything.

Build the idea

Platelets grab the torn edge

A tear exposes collagen, tough fibres under the smooth lining. Platelets that touch it turn spiky and sticky and cling on, helped by von Willebrand factor, a blood protein that acts like double-sided tape between platelet and collagen.

Build the idea

Step two: a platelet plug

Stuck platelets release chemicals, such as ADP and thromboxane, that call in more platelets, which pile on. Together they form a platelet plug: a soft, temporary seal over a small tear, like the wooden bung a ship’s crew hammers into a leak.

Build the idea

Step three: the plug needs a net

A plug of platelets alone is soft. Clotting knits it into a tough patch. Blood carries fibrinogen, a dissolved protein. Turned into fibrin, long sticky threads, it weaves a net through the plug. Something has to flip that switch.

Build the idea

Clotting factors: a chain of switches

Clotting factors are proteins, most made by the liver, that float in blood switched off. One switched on turns on the next, and that one the next: a cascade, like water dropping down a waterfall. Each step turns on more.

Build the idea

Two ways in, one shared path

A tear exposes tissue factor on cells outside the vessel, which with factor VII starts the fast extrinsic pathway. Contact inside the vessel starts the slower intrinsic pathway, through factors XII, XI and IX. Both switch on factor X (ten), the first step of a shared common pathway.

Build the idea

Factor ten makes the net-builder

Switched-on factor X, written Xa (say “ten-a”), works with calcium on the platelets’ surface to turn prothrombin (factor II), an idle blood protein, into thrombin: an enzyme, a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction.

Build the idea

Thrombin spins the fibrin net

Thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin threads. They weave through the platelet plug and trap red blood cells, and factor XIII (thirteen) ties the threads together into a firm net. Platelets then pull the threads tight, like lacing a shoe.

Build the idea

A natural brake: antithrombin

Antithrombin is a blood protein that acts as a brake. It switches off thrombin and factor Xa, so the clot stays at the wound instead of spreading along the vessel. A heparin-like coat on the healthy vessel lining speeds it up.

Build the idea

Clean-up: plasmin cuts the net away

As the wall heals, the clot must go. Plasminogen, an idle blood protein, is switched on into plasmin, an enzyme that gradually breaks down the fibrin threads. This slow clean-up is called fibrinolysis.

Build the idea

The whole picture: plug and net

Squeeze, plug, net. Platelets plug the tear; the cascade switches on factor X, then thrombin, which spins fibrin through the plug. Two things to watch: how many platelets there are, the platelet count, and how much thrombin gets made.

What changes it

Heparins: boosting the antithrombin brake

Heparins are anticoagulants, drugs that oppose clotting. They work through antithrombin: heparin latches on and makes it switch off thrombin and factor Xa far faster. Some newer heparins act mostly on factor Xa. With less thrombin, the fibrin net thins.

What changes it

Vitamin K antagonists: faulty switches

The liver needs vitamin K to finish factors II, VII, IX and X, and the brakes proteins C and S. Each use spends the vitamin, so it is recycled. Vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin block that recycling, so the liver releases factors that cannot work. With fewer working switches, less thrombin and fibrin are made.

What changes it

Factor Xa inhibitors: jamming factor X

Factor Xa inhibitors act directly on switched-on factor X, without needing antithrombin. The shared path stalls at its first step, so less thrombin is made and the fibrin net stays thin.

What changes it

Direct thrombin inhibitors: blocking thrombin

Direct thrombin inhibitors act on thrombin itself. Factor X still switches on, but thrombin’s work is held back, so fibrinogen is not turned into fibrin and the net barely forms. The platelet plug is left without its net.

What changes it

Antiplatelet drugs: less sticky platelets

Antiplatelet drugs stop platelets calling in more platelets. Aspirin stops them making thromboxane; P2Y12 blockers, such as clopidogrel, block the landing site for ADP. Fewer platelets pile on, so the plug is smaller and looser, and the cascade has less platelet surface to work on.

What changes it

Thrombolytics: dissolving the net

Thrombolytics are clot-busting drugs. One, tissue plasminogen activator, is an enzyme the vessel lining releases naturally; given as a drug, it turns plasminogen into plasmin, which cuts up the fibrin net of an unwanted clot blocking a vessel, so blood can flow again.

      How this teaching model works

      Everything is relative to a normal clot, which scores 1 on every part. The platelet plug grows with the platelet count but levels off (count × 1.5 ÷ (count + 0.5)), and antiplatelet drugs shrink it. Factor X’s activity falls when vitamin K antagonists leave fewer working factors, when a factor Xa inhibitor jams it, and when heparin speeds up the antithrombin brake. Thrombin follows factor X, scaled by how much platelet surface the plug offers, and falls further with heparins (antithrombin again) and direct thrombin inhibitors. The fibrin net follows thrombin, and thrombolytics then cut most of it away.

      The sizes of every effect are illustrative choices; only their directions are meant to teach. Left out: vascular spasm’s timing, factor XIII, the work of proteins C and S (drawn, but not modelled) and tissue factor pathway inhibitor (two more natural brakes), time, and every drug amount.
