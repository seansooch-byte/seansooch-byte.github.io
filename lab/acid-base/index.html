The Lab / Unit 3: Breath and blood / Lesson 11

Acid, base
and balance.

Your body makes acid all day, yet the blood barely notices. Build up how a chemical buffer, your lungs and your kidneys share the job, then watch two kinds of diuretic, sodium bicarbonate and an aspirin overdose tip the balance.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the idea

Blood has to stay in a narrow band

pH is a scale of how acidic or alkaline a liquid is. Lemon juice is acidic; soap is alkaline, the opposite of acidic. Water sits in the middle. Blood sits just on the alkaline side, and the body holds it inside a narrow band.

Build the idea

Your body makes acid all day

Working cells burn fuel and give off carbon dioxide, a waste gas, and other acids, such as lactic acid from hard-working muscle. An acid releases hydrogen ions: tiny charged particles that push pH toward acidic. Something has to deal with them.

Build the idea

A buffer soaks up the shock

A buffer is a pair of chemicals that grabs extra acid, or extra base (anything that mops up hydrogen ions), so pH barely moves. Drop acid into plain water and pH swings a long way; drop it into blood and it hardly shifts.

Build the idea

The bicarbonate buffer

Bicarbonate is a base dissolved in blood. When it catches a hydrogen ion it becomes carbonic acid, a weak acid that holds on to that ion instead of letting it loose. Blood keeps far more bicarbonate than carbonic acid, ready for incoming acid.

Build the idea

Carbonic acid and carbon dioxide trade places

Carbonic acid easily splits into carbon dioxide and water, and the two can join back. Carbonic anhydrase, an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction), makes this swap fast. So carbon dioxide in blood acts as acid, and breathing it out removes acid.

Build the idea

pH is a balance of two sides

Picture a seesaw. On one side, carbon dioxide, the acid side; on the other, bicarbonate, the base side. pH depends on the balance between them, not on the amount of either. At the normal mix, about twenty bicarbonate to each dissolved carbon dioxide, the beam sits level and pH stays in its band.

Build the idea

The lungs set the acid side

Breathing faster or deeper blows off more carbon dioxide, lightening the acid side so pH rises. Breathing slower lets carbon dioxide build up, so pH falls. Sensors in the arteries and brain adjust breathing within minutes (more on the lungs in Lesson 10).

Build the idea

The kidneys set the base side

The kidneys control bicarbonate. Cells lining their tubes push hydrogen ions out into the urine and return bicarbonate to the blood, using carbonic anhydrase. They can keep more or let more go, but this takes hours to days (Lesson 2 covers the kidney’s tubes).

Build the idea

Three defences, three speeds

Buffers act in seconds, catching acid the moment it appears. The lungs respond in minutes by changing breathing. The kidneys are slowest, taking hours to days, but they clear acids that breathing cannot, and rebuild bicarbonate.

Build the idea

Four ways it goes wrong

A process that pushes blood toward acid is an acidosis; toward alkaline, an alkalosis. Blood that ends up too acidic or too alkaline has acidemia or alkalemia. If a breathing problem changed the carbon dioxide first, it is respiratory. If bicarbonate changed first, it is metabolic. Two directions, two causes: four disorders.

Build the idea

The other side compensates

When one side goes wrong, the other side moves the same way, to bring the balance back. This is compensation. Here bicarbonate fell first, so the lungs breathe off extra carbon dioxide. pH comes back toward normal, but compensation never pushes it past normal.

Build the idea

The whole picture: three things to read

Buffers catch acid at once, the lungs set carbon dioxide, the kidneys set bicarbonate. A blood test reads three key measures: pH says whether the blood is too acidic or too alkaline; carbon dioxide and bicarbonate, read against what compensation alone would give, say whether the cause is respiratory or metabolic, whether compensation has begun, or whether a second disorder is hiding.

What changes it

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors: bicarbonate leaks away

Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, such as acetazolamide, are diuretics, drugs that make the kidneys pass more urine. They block carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule (Lesson 2), so bicarbonate is lost in urine. Bicarbonate falls first, a metabolic acidosis, and the lungs then partly compensate.

What changes it

Loop and thiazide diuretics: contraction alkalosis

Loop and thiazide diuretics make the kidneys pass extra salt and water but little bicarbonate, so the same bicarbonate sits in less fluid: contraction alkalosis. The lost volume also calls up angiotensin II and aldosterone, which make the kidney keep bicarbonate and push out hydrogen ions and potassium; low potassium adds to it (Lesson 2).

What changes it

Sodium bicarbonate: adding base

Sodium bicarbonate, also found in some antacids for heartburn, is bicarbonate itself. Taken in, it adds straight to the base side, so blood tips toward alkalosis. Breathing slows a little, though the lungs answer an alkalosis less well, and healthy kidneys soon pass the extra bicarbonate, so the swing is usually short.

What changes it

Salicylate toxicity: breathing first, then acid

Salicylate toxicity means too much aspirin in the body. First it drives the brain’s breathing centre hard, blowing off carbon dioxide: a respiratory alkalosis. Then it upsets how cells make energy, so acids such as lactic acid build up and use up bicarbonate: a metabolic acidosis joins in. With both at once, pH can look near normal.

    How this teaching model works

    The buffer reactions, which the lesson draws as pictures: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3–. Carbon dioxide and water form carbonic acid (carbonic anhydrase speeds this up), and carbonic acid dissociates into a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate; every step runs both ways.

    The model uses the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for the bicarbonate buffer: pH = 6.1 + log10(HCO3 ÷ (0.03 × PCO2)), with bicarbonate in mM and carbon dioxide in mm Hg. The two constants, 6.1 and 0.03, belong to the equation; they are not from the cited excerpts. Its baseline carbon dioxide sits at the centre of OpenStax’s male and female ranges taken together, and its baseline bicarbonate is set so the bicarbonate to carbonic acid ratio is OpenStax’s 20 to 1, which gives a pH inside OpenStax’s normal range. The seesaw shows each side as six tokens at that normal mix, so a token stands for a sixth of the normal amount, not for single molecules.

    The two top sliders set the first change as a multiple of the baseline. Compensation then moves the other side in the same direction as the first change, a fraction of the way toward the value that would restore the normal ratio: up to three quarters of the way when the lungs answer an acidosis, half when they answer an alkalosis (OpenStax notes they are less efficient at that), and seven tenths when the kidneys answer a respiratory change. Because it never goes all the way, compensation can bring pH back toward normal but never past it. If both sides have been pushed toward the same disorder there is nothing left to compensate with, and if they already pull in opposite directions the model adds nothing more.

    Left out: the protein and phosphate buffers, time (the compensation slider stands in for minutes in the lungs and hours to days in the kidneys), potassium and chloride, and limits on how far real breathing or kidneys can go. The sizes of the drug and disease changes in the lesson are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.
