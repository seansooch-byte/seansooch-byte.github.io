The Lab / Unit 6: Inflammation, infection, immunity and cancer / Lesson 18

Inflammation and the arachidonic acid pathway.

When a cell is hurt, it snips a fatty tail out of its own skin and turns it into a burst of signals: some protect you, some bring pain, swelling and fever. Watch that pathway flow, one piece at a time, then see five kinds of anti-inflammatory drugs block it, each in a different place.

Educational, not for clinical use

The trigger

Cells and their membrane

Your body is built from cells, tiny living units. Each cell is wrapped in a thin skin, its membrane. The membrane is made of phospholipids: fat molecules with a water-loving head and two oily tails, packed side by side in a double layer.

The trigger

An injury sets off inflammation

When part of the body is hurt by a knock, a cut or germs, it answers with inflammation: the area turns red, warm, swollen and painful. Inflammation brings help to clear the damage. Among its first signals are ones made from the cell membrane itself.

The trigger

Scissors free arachidonic acid

Injury switches on an enzyme, a protein that speeds up one chemical change. This one, phospholipase A2, works like scissors: it snips a fatty tail called arachidonic acid free from the membrane’s phospholipids. Every signal that follows is built from it.

The signals

Two roads from one raw material

Freed arachidonic acid can take two roads. One runs through cyclooxygenase, called COX for short; the other runs through lipoxygenase. Both are enzymes, and each turns arachidonic acid into a different family of signals.

The signals

COX-1 keeps the stomach shield up

COX-1 is the everyday form of COX, at work in most tissues all the time. It makes prostaglandins, short-lived local signals. In the stomach they keep up the stomach shield: they brake acid and thicken the mucus and bicarbonate over the lining (Lesson 16).

The signals

Platelets and thromboxane

Platelets are tiny cell pieces in the blood that clump together to plug a leak in a blood vessel (Lesson 12). Their COX-1 makes thromboxane, a signal that calls more platelets to stick and clump and narrows the vessel.

The signals

COX-2: the injury enzyme

COX-2 is the second form of COX. Most tissues hold little of it, though the kidneys, brain and blood vessel linings keep some. Injured tissue and the white cells it calls in quickly make much more, and at the injury COX-2 pours out the prostaglandins that drive inflammation.

The signals

Pain, swelling and fever

These prostaglandins widen nearby blood vessels and help them leak, so the area reddens and swells. They make pain nerves more sensitive. In the brain they turn up the body’s thermostat, causing fever: a raised body temperature. From here on, one meter tracks all three: pain, swelling and fever.

The signals

Lipoxygenase makes leukotrienes

Down the other road, lipoxygenase makes leukotrienes. They call in white blood cells, the body’s defence cells, and squeeze the airways, the breathing tubes of the lungs. Too much of them helps narrow the airways in asthma, a lung condition with wheezy breathing.

The signals

The whole pathway: the key measures

Injury frees arachidonic acid. COX-1 keeps up the stomach shield and platelet clumping; COX-2 drives pain, swelling and fever; lipoxygenase makes leukotrienes, which squeeze the airways. Two key measures sum it up: how much pain, swelling and fever, and how strong the stomach shield is.

What changes it

Ordinary NSAIDs: both COX enzymes plugged

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, not steroids), such as ibuprofen, are the ordinary kind: they plug both COX-1 and COX-2 for a while. Pain, swelling and fever fall, but the stomach shield thins and platelets clump less. A little arachidonic acid spills toward leukotrienes, which can worsen some people’s asthma.

What changes it

COX-2 selective inhibitors: only COX-2 plugged

COX-2 selective inhibitors, such as celecoxib, plug COX-2 but leave COX-1 working. Pain, swelling and fever fall, while the stomach shield and platelet clumping stay near normal. But blood vessel linings use COX-2 to make prostacyclin, which keeps platelets from sticking; losing it raises the risk of heart attack and stroke, and the kidneys still need COX-2 too.

What changes it

Aspirin: COX tagged for good

Aspirin blocks COX for good by sticking a chemical tag, an acetyl group, onto it. Most cells can build new COX, but platelets have no nucleus and cannot, so their thromboxane stays low and clumping stays down for the rest of each platelet’s life. Pain and swelling ease; the stomach shield thins.

What changes it

Glucocorticoids: the scissors slowed

Glucocorticoids, steroid medicines modelled on the stress hormone cortisol (Lesson 15), such as prednisone, work higher up. They make cells produce annexin A1 (once called lipocortin), which holds back phospholipase A2, and stop cells making extra COX-2, so prostaglandins and leukotrienes both fall.

What changes it

Leukotriene modifiers: the airway squeeze eased

Leukotriene modifiers either block leukotrienes where they act on the airways, as montelukast does, or block lipoxygenase itself. The airway squeeze eases, which helps prevent asthma attacks. COX and its prostaglandins carry on untouched, so pain and fever stay, though leukotriene-driven airway swelling eases too.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is worked out relative to the untreated injury and settles at once; there is no time course. Phospholipase frees a trickle of arachidonic acid at rest and more as the injury grows. COX-1 is always present; COX-2 is made in proportion to the injury. At the injury COX-2 does most of the work, so the prostaglandins behind pain, swelling and fever follow COX-2 far more than COX-1. The stomach shield and platelet clumping follow COX-1 alone. Arachidonic acid that COX cannot take spills a little toward lipoxygenase, so blocking COX raises leukotrienes slightly.

    An NSAID plugs a share of both COX enzymes; a COX-2 selective inhibitor plugs COX-2 only; aspirin tags COX-1 almost completely and COX-2 less so, and because platelets cannot make new COX its effect on them lasts. A glucocorticoid slows phospholipase, stops much of the extra COX-2 being made and damps inflammation in other ways too. A leukotriene modifier blocks leukotrienes where they act, so the airway squeeze falls while leukotrienes are still made. The sizes of every effect are illustrative; only their directions are meant to teach.

    Left out: prostacyclin, the blood-vessel prostaglandin whose loss under COX-2 selective inhibitors is part of their heart risk (the lesson names it, the model does not draw it); the kidney’s prostaglandins; other routes for arachidonic acid; the many other inflammatory signals; how drugs are absorbed and cleared; and every real-world difference between people. Nothing here is a prediction for a real person, and nothing here is a treatment plan.
