The Lab / Unit 2: Nerves, rhythm and muscle / Lesson 8

The neuromuscular
junction.

Every movement you make starts as a nerve signal that has to jump a gap too small to see. Follow one signal across it into a muscle, then watch how paralysing drugs, the drugs that reverse them, and one disease change the crossing.

Educational, not for clinical use

Build the signal

Muscles move only when a nerve tells them

Skeletal muscles, the ones that move your bones, act only on orders from the brain and spinal cord. A motor nerve carries them: a cable of axons, thin wires each reaching out from one nerve cell in the brainstem or spinal cord to the muscle.

Build the signal

Each nerve ending meets one muscle fibre

A muscle is a bundle of long, thin cells called muscle fibres. The motor nerve branches, and each branch ends near the middle of one fibre. That meeting point is the neuromuscular junction. This lesson zooms into it.

Build the signal

A gap, and a folded landing pad

The nerve ending never touches the fibre. A tiny gap, the synaptic gap, separates them. Under the gap, the fibre’s surface is pleated into deep folds. This patch is the end plate, and it packs a lot of surface into a small space.

Build the signal

The messenger waits in packets

The signal crosses the gap as a chemical. The nerve ending makes acetylcholine, the same messenger as the brake nerves in Lesson 6, and stores it in vesicles: tiny bubbles, each holding thousands of acetylcholine molecules.

Build the signal

A nerve impulse spills the packets

A nerve impulse is the electrical signal racing down the nerve. When it reaches the ending, calcium doors open and calcium flows in. Calcium makes vesicles merge with the ending’s wall and spill their acetylcholine into the gap.

Build the signal

Receptors are gates that acetylcholine opens

The end plate is studded with acetylcholine receptors: gates in the fibre’s surface. These are nicotinic receptors, a different kind from the organs’ muscarinic ones. A gate opens only while two acetylcholine molecules are docked in it, and an open gate lets sodium rush into the fibre.

Build the signal

Sodium makes the end plate’s voltage jump

At rest, the inside of a fibre is negative compared with the outside; that difference is its voltage. Incoming sodium makes the inside less negative, a change called depolarization. This brief bump at the end plate is the end plate potential.

Build the signal

A wide margin of safety

To fire, the fibre’s voltage must reach a set level, its threshold. A normal end plate potential overshoots the threshold by a wide margin, so every impulse fires the fibre. That spare room is the safety margin.

Build the signal

The fibre fires, then twitches

Reaching threshold sets off a muscle action potential: a wave of voltage that sweeps along the whole fibre and makes it shorten. One impulse gives one quick contraction, a twitch. Then the end plate’s voltage settles back, ready for the next impulse.

Build the signal

An enzyme wipes the gap clean

Cholinesterase, an enzyme waiting in the folds, chops acetylcholine apart within moments, so the gates close and each impulse gives just one twitch. Lesson 6 met the same enzyme at the brake nerves’ synapses.

Build the signal

Quick bursts test the margin

Fire the nerve several times in quick succession and each impulse releases a little less acetylcholine than the one before. The safety margin absorbs the drop: every end plate potential still clears threshold, so the twitches stay equally strong. Anesthetists test the junction with such a burst of four, the train of four.

Build the signal

The whole picture

Impulse, calcium, acetylcholine, open gates, an end plate potential above threshold, a fired fibre, a twitch; then cholinesterase resets the gap. Two key measures: how far each end plate potential clears threshold, and whether the four twitches of a quick burst stay equally strong.

What changes it

Nondepolarizing blockers: gates jammed shut

A neuromuscular blocker is a drug that stops muscles contracting, as during surgery. Nondepolarizing blockers, such as rocuronium, sit in the gates without opening them, crowding acetylcholine out. End plate potentials fall below threshold in many fibres: twitches weaken, and fade through a burst.

What changes it

Succinylcholine: the end plate held depolarized

Succinylcholine, a depolarizing blocker, is two acetylcholine molecules joined. It opens the gates again and again, and the junction’s cholinesterase cannot break it down, so the end plate stays depolarized: a brief flurry of small twitches, then the sodium doors around it lock and the fibre cannot fire. This phase I block shrinks twitches evenly, without fade, until an enzyme in the blood clears it.

What changes it

Cholinesterase inhibitors: reversing the block

An inhibitor stops an enzyme. After a nondepolarizing blocker, an inhibitor such as neostigmine lets acetylcholine pile up, so it wins gates back and twitches recover. A muscarinic antagonist is given with it so the heart does not slow (Lesson 6). It deepens succinylcholine’s phase I block instead, and too much can itself cause weakness.

What changes it

Myasthenia gravis: too few gates

In most myasthenia gravis, the immune system makes antibodies, proteins that tag a target for attack, against acetylcholine receptors, blocking and destroying them (in a few, against other junction proteins). With fewer gates, end plate potentials barely clear threshold, so muscles tire with use and twitches fade. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as pyridostigmine ease it; too much causes weakness of its own.

    How this teaching model works

    Everything is relative to a normal junction. Acetylcholine in the gap is the amount released divided by how fast cholinesterase clears it. A nondepolarizing blocker competes with acetylcholine for the same receptors: the share of receptors opened by acetylcholine is its level divided by its level plus a constant scaled up by the blocker. Opened receptors, times the share of receptors still present, give the end plate potential, which levels off as more gates open because the voltage cannot rise forever. The safety margin is the end plate potential compared with threshold; the model takes a normal junction’s margin from the textbook safety factor in the Clinical values data file. Fibres differ a little, so as the margin shrinks some fibres miss first and the twitch weakens gradually rather than all at once. In a quick burst of impulses each impulse releases a little less acetylcholine; a nondepolarizing blocker also sits on the nerve ending’s own receptors and steepens that drop, which is why its twitches fade. Succinylcholine is treated as holding the end plate depolarized, so in a share of fibres the surrounding membrane cannot reset and that fibre does not fire at all, however much acetylcholine arrives; the other fibres keep their full margin, so its twitches shrink evenly without fading, and extra acetylcholine from a cholinesterase inhibitor deepens that first, held-open stage of block. Myasthenia gravis is shown as fewer receptors. The model leaves out calcium handling inside the muscle, the second phase of a long succinylcholine block, the weakness that too much cholinesterase inhibitor causes on its own, the drugs’ effects elsewhere in the body, and all timing; drug strengths are arbitrary, so only directions are meant to teach. In the Clinical values tab the relative results are scaled to the textbook values, as that tab explains.
